# Krar Lab: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 8 hours, best run as five sessions of about 1.5 hours, for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. Everything happens indoors at tables. Suggested sessions: (1) steps 1–2, (2) step 3, (3) step 4, (4) step 5 and the frame part of step 6, (5) the rest of step 6 and step 7.

---

## Before the module

### 1. Shopping and local sources

| Item | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nylon fishing line, 0.3, 0.5 and 0.8 mm (a 0.6 or 0.7 mm reel is a bonus) | Fishing and sports shops in Merkato; lake towns; music shops that sell krars | Check the diameter on the label. Clear monofilament is best. Buy plenty: lines break and kink. |
| Planks about 80 × 10 × 2 cm, one per team | Carpenter's offcuts | Any flat, straight wood. |
| Eucalyptus sticks or straight battens, 2–3 cm thick | Wood sellers, carpenters | Per team: two arms of 55 cm, a yoke of 35 cm, a tail bar of 25 cm. |
| Boxes or tins | Carpenters, shops, home | Small wooden boxes sound best; biscuit tins are loud and bright; cardboard works if the frame takes the load. |
| Eye bolts M5 or M6 × 60 mm, wing nuts, washers | Hardware shops | 5 per team plus spares. |
| Wood screws, cord, wood glue, duct tape | Hardware shops | |
| Plastic water bottles | Collect | At least two each of 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 L per team. |
| Kitchen scale, measuring jug | Household shops | One scale for the class is enough. |
| Safety glasses | Hardware or safety shops | One per student. |

Prices change quickly, so check them yourself. The whole kit for four teams is mostly fishing line, bolts and wing nuts; wood offcuts are often free.

### 2. Phones and apps

Install a free **chromatic tuner** app that shows the frequency in Hz (not only the note name), and a free **sound-level meter** app, on at least one phone per team, over Wi-Fi before the first session. Test the tuner on a plucked line: some apps need a fairly loud, steady note and will jump between readings on a quiet one. Pick the app that gives the steadiest number. If you can, use the same app on every phone so teams' results compare.

### 3. Guide-built parts (the week before)

**Monochord planks.** Drive one wood screw into each plank, 3 cm from one end, leaving 1 cm standing. Cut 5 cm lengths of smooth 20–25 mm plastic pipe for the far bridges. Build one complete monochord yourself and check it reads steadily before students arrive; you need it for the step 1 demonstration.

**Krar frames.** For each team:

- cut two arms (55 cm), a yoke (35 cm) and a tail bar (25 cm),
- drill five holes through the yoke, about 5 cm apart, just wider than the eye-bolt thread,
- pre-drill screw holes where the bars meet the arms (two screws per joint), so students only drive the screws,
- cut a bridge strip about 20–25 cm long and 1.5 cm tall from hard wood, with five small notches 4–5 cm apart (to match the yoke spacing roughly).

**Tins.** If a team uses a tin, cut or file any sharp edges and cover every edge with two layers of duct tape before handing it over.

### 4. Your own krar

Bring a real krar if you can, or a recording, for step 1. If you have one, a krar player visiting for step 7 is a wonderful finish.

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1, repeat whenever strings are loaded)

- Glasses on whenever a string is under tension. A snapping nylon line whips back fast and can hit an eye.
- Weight limits: 0.3 mm line, 2 kg maximum; 0.5 mm and thicker, 3 kg maximum. Typical breaking loads are about 4–5 kg for 0.3 mm and 10 kg or more for 0.5 mm, but knots weaken a line by up to half, so these limits keep a margin.
- A catch-bucket filled with crumpled paper under every hanging bottle, no more than 10 cm below it. Feet out from under it.
- Lower bottles slowly. Never let one drop onto the line.
- Only you use the saw and the drill.
- Tighten tuners a quarter-turn at a time; nobody looks along a string from close up while tightening.
- No mains electricity anywhere in the module.

## Session plans

### Step 1: The singing line (40 min)

Demonstrate with your own monochord. The sequence matters: loose line (dull buzz, the tuner can't read it), 1 L, 2 L (clearly higher), pencil under the line (higher), plank on a box (louder). Don't explain yet.

Students usually predict that halving the length doubles the pitch (correct) and that doubling the weight doubles the pitch (wrong: it raises it by about 41%). Keep the guesses; they come back in step 7.

### Step 2: Build a one-string box (75 min)

**Expected readings** with 0.5 mm nylon, a 70 cm vibrating length and a 1.5 kg bottle (14.7 N): about 180–190 Hz. Readings should agree within 2–3 Hz once the bottle is still and the line has stretched.

**Where students get stuck.**

- The line rubs on the table edge: the far bridge must be well past the edge.
- Readings creep downward over the first few minutes: the nylon is stretching. Wait two minutes after first loading.
- The tuner jumps an octave (shows double or half the frequency): pluck in the middle of the vibrating part, not near a bridge; plucking near a bridge gives strong overtones.
- Plucking hard gives the same frequency but a louder note: point this out; it is the difference between amplitude and frequency.

### Step 3: Shorter is higher (85 min)

**Ruler.** At 20 cm overhang a 30 cm plastic ruler vibrates at roughly 10–20 Hz, visible as a blur and countable in slow motion; shorter overhangs are faster and start to hum.

**Theory for the answer key.** For a string, $f = \dfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$, where $\mu$ is the mass per metre. Students don't need the formula; they discover each part of it.

**Expected results** (0.5 mm nylon, $\mu \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-4}$ kg/m, 1.5 kg bottle so $T = 14.7$ N, wave speed on the line about 260 m/s):

| $L$ (m) | expected $f$ (Hz) | $1/L$ (1/m) | $f \times L$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.60 | 215 | 1.67 | 129 |
| 0.50 | 260 | 2.00 | 130 |
| 0.40 | 325 | 2.50 | 130 |
| 0.30 | 430 | 3.33 | 129 |
| 0.25 | 520 | 4.00 | 130 |
| 0.20 | 650 | 5.00 | 130 |

Real lines vary, so students' numbers may be 10–20% different, but $f \times L$ should stay roughly constant (within about 5%). The gradient of $f$ against $1/L$ equals $f \times L$, about 130 Hz·m. (For keen students: the gradient is half the wave speed on the string.)

**Prediction example.** For 440 Hz: $L = 130 \div 440 = 0.295$ m, about 29.5 cm. Most teams land within 2–4%; anything within 5% meets the sign-off.

**Periods.** 260 Hz → 3.8 ms; 650 Hz → 1.5 ms; 215 Hz → 4.7 ms.

**Where students get stuck.** Plucking on the wrong side of the movable pen. Measuring length from the screw instead of the fixed bridge. Plotting $f$ against $L$ and forcing a straight line through a curve. Not starting the $1/L$ axis at zero, so they can't see whether the line goes through the origin.

**Questions to ask.** "If $f \times L$ is always 130, what length gives 1000 Hz?" (13 cm.) "Why can't a string be made to play 1 Hz?" (It would need to be 130 m long at this tension.)

### Step 4: Tighter and thicker (85 min)

**Expected results, test A** (0.5 mm line, $L = 0.50$ m):

| Mass (kg) | Tension (N) | expected $f$ (Hz) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 4.9 | about 150 |
| 1.0 | 9.8 | about 210 |
| 1.5 | 14.7 | about 260 |
| 2.0 | 19.6 | about 300 |
| 3.0 | 29.4 | about 360 |

Four times the mass (0.5 → 2.0 kg) doubles the frequency (150 → 300 Hz). The $f$ against $T$ graph is a curve that bends over; $f^2$ against $T$ is a straight line through the origin (the challenge). Friction where the line passes over the pipe makes the real tension a little less than $mg$; readings may be a few percent low.

**Expected results, test B** ($L = 0.50$ m, 1.5 kg):

| $d$ (mm) | expected $f$ (Hz) | $f \times d$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | about 425 | about 128 |
| 0.5 | about 260 | about 130 |
| 0.8 | about 160 | about 128 |

The line's mass per metre goes up with the square of the diameter, so $f$ is inversely proportional to $d$: $f \times d$ stays about the same. Different brands of nylon differ a little, so expect 10% scatter.

**Controlled variables** students should list: length, the line (for test A), the weight (for test B), the plucking position, the phone and app, and waiting for the bottle to stop swinging.

**Where students get stuck.** Changing length and weight together. Forgetting to weigh the bottle (a 2 L bottle with water is about 2.05 kg). Swapping lines without re-measuring the length.

### Step 5: The box that sings (50 min)

**Expected results.** With the phone 30 cm away, a plank lifted on cups typically reads only a few dB above the room's background; on a box it is usually 5–15 dB louder. Wooden boxes and tins are louder than cardboard. The note **dies faster** on the box: the string's energy is passed into the box and the air more quickly, so it is louder for a shorter time. On cups the note may ring for 5–10 s; on a box, a few seconds. A noisy room ruins this test: do it when other groups are quiet, and measure the background level first.

**The key idea.** The string is too thin to push much air. Its vibration passes through the bridge into the box's large surface, which pushes much more air. The box adds no energy; it spreads the string's energy into the air faster. A box (and the air inside it) has natural frequencies of its own; notes near them are especially loud, which is why some strings on a krar ring louder than others.

**Paper riders.** When both lines are tuned the same, the paper on line B jumps or falls off: line A's vibration travels through the box and pushes line B in time with its natural frequency. When they're detuned, the paper barely moves. At half the length (double the frequency), the paper often moves a little too, because line A's sound contains a component at twice its frequency. Mention this lightly; it isn't needed for the sign-off.

**Where students get stuck.** Plucking with different strength each time (insist on the 1 cm pull). Saying "the box makes energy". Ask: "Where could that energy come from?"

### Step 6: Build and tune your krar (105 min)

This is the longest step; split it over two sessions if needed (frame and stringing in one, tuning in the next, so the nylon can stretch overnight, which makes tuning much easier).

**Frame tips.** The closed frame (arms, yoke, tail bar) must take all the string tension; five strings pull with roughly 50–100 N in total. Glue plus two screws per joint. A cardboard box must not carry the load; it only sits in the frame. Make sure the bridge stands high enough that every string presses on it.

**Ratios.** Tizita major uses the notes of the major pentatonic scale (in C: C D E G A); bati major uses 1, 3, 4, 5, 7 of the major scale (in C: C E F G B). The table uses simple whole-number ratios (9/8, 5/4, 3/2, 5/3 and 5/4, 4/3, 3/2, 15/8). Tuner apps use equal temperament, which differs from these ratios by less than 1%, well inside the 2% target. Traditional Ethiopian tunings vary from player to player and region to region; the point here is the ratio method, not a single "correct" tuning. Other qenets (anchihoye, ambassel, and the minor forms of tizita and bati) are good extensions for keen teams.

**Target frequencies.**

| Start note | Tizita targets (Hz) | Bati targets (Hz) |
|---|---|---|
| C, 262 Hz | 262, 295, 328, 393, 437 | 262, 328, 349, 393, 491 |
| G, 196 Hz | 196, 221, 245, 294, 327 | 196, 245, 261, 294, 368 |

Equal-tempered values for C tizita are 262, 294, 330, 392, 440; for C bati 262, 330, 349, 392, 494.

**String choice.** For a vibrating length of about 40–45 cm, a 0.5 mm line reaches 262 Hz at about 10–13 N (similar to hanging 1–1.3 kg), a 0.4 mm line reaches 440 Hz at about 18–22 N, and a 0.8 mm line needs about 30 N for 262 Hz but only about 18 N for 196 Hz. Strings that need much more tension than that are near their limit: go thinner or start lower. The G start is the safer choice for cardboard boxes and weaker frames.

**Periods** for the example: 262 Hz → 3.8 ms; 437 Hz → 2.3 ms.

**Where students get stuck.** Strings tied too slack, so the wing nut runs out of thread before the note is reached: retie hand-tight. Eye bolts spinning instead of pulling: hold the eye with pliers while turning the wing nut. Tuning one string, then finding it flat after tuning the others: the frame flexes as tension builds, so go round several times.

### Step 7: Krar concert (45 min)

Expect strings to have drifted 5–15 Hz flat since step 6. Keep the concert short and celebratory; do the sign-off conversations team by team while others practise.

## Questions to ask throughout

- "What did you change, and what did you keep the same?"
- "Your reading jumped. What might have caused it?"
- "If the straight line went through the origin, what does that tell you?"
- "Where is the energy going when the note dies away?"
- "Why do you think a krar's lowest string is the thickest one, rather than just the longest?" (A longer string would need a bigger instrument.)

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **monochord:** a steady reading (within about 3 Hz on repeats), a clear vibrating length, and a catch-bucket in place.
- **length-graph:** at least five lengths, three readings each, means correct; axes labelled with units and starting at zero; one straight line through (or very close to) the origin, not dot-to-dot; a prediction worked from the graph or the gradient, checked, with the percentage error within 5%.
- **fair-test:** controlled variables listed and really kept the same (same length for both tests, same weight in test B). The tension pattern stated as "four times the weight, double the frequency" or equivalent (or "the curve bends over, the frequency rises more slowly than the weight" for a weaker but acceptable answer). Thickness: thicker gives lower, ideally with $f \times d$ roughly constant.
- **resonance:** numbers for with and without the box. The explanation must say the string's vibration is passed into the box, which moves much more air; the box adds no energy. Mentioning the shorter ringing time earns extra credit, not required.
- **tuned-krar:** check each string with your own tuner. All five within 2% of the team's own targets, and still close after the performance.
- **plays:** a recognisable pattern or tune, and one ratio explained (for example "string 4 is 1.5 times string 1").
- **safety:** glasses and catch-buckets every time. If a student broke a rule after a reminder, discuss it with them before signing.
