## Do: with and without the box (20 minutes)

Use your monochord with the 0.5 mm line, 50 cm long, 1.5 kg hanging. Glasses on.

Put a phone with a **sound-level (decibel) meter** app 30 cm above the middle of the line. Pluck the same way each time: pull the line 1 cm sideways with a fingertip and let go.

Test four ways, three plucks each:

1. plank lifted off the table on two cups (no box),
2. plank flat on the table,
3. plank on top of an empty cardboard box,
4. plank on top of a tin or wooden box.

For each pluck, write the **loudest reading** (dB) and **how long** you can hear the note (stopwatch, seconds).

## Notice something puzzling

The box doesn't have a battery. It can't add energy. So how can the string be louder on the box? And look at your "seconds heard" column: is the loudest setup also the longest-lasting?

## Theory: resonance

Read the first section of **Resonance and tuning**, *Everything has a natural frequency* (the coil-and-capacitor part belongs to the Radio module; you can skim it). You'll learn:

- that every object has a **natural frequency**,
- that small pushes in time with that frequency build a big vibration,
- that this is called **resonance**.

## Do: one string plays another (20 minutes)

Two teams put their monochords side by side on the same box, or on one table.

1. Tune both lines to the **same** frequency (move a bridge until the tuner shows the same number).
2. Fold a tiny V of paper (1 cm) and sit it on the middle of team B's line.
3. Team A plucks **their** line. Watch team B's paper.
4. Now move team B's bridge so their line is a different note. Try again.
5. Try team B at **half** the length of team A (twice the frequency). What happens?

## Record (10 minutes)

Answer in full sentences:

1. Why is a plucked line quiet on its own? (Hint: how much air can a thin line push?)
2. How does the box make it louder? Use the words *vibration*, *resonance* and *energy*.
3. Why does the note die away faster on the box?
4. Why did the paper only jump when the two lines matched?
