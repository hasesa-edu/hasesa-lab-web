## The standard recipe (10 minutes)

To compare dyes fairly, every extract is made the same way. Only the plant changes.

| Plant | Mass in 500 mL water |
|---|---|
| Dry onion skins | 20 g |
| Turmeric (ird), ground | 10 g |
| Dried karkade | 20 g |
| Red cabbage, chopped | 100 g |
| Used coffee grounds | 50 g |
| Black tea or fresh eucalyptus leaves | 20 g tea, or 50 g eucalyptus torn small |

Some masses are different because some plants are mostly water (cabbage) or very strong (turmeric). Write down why that still lets you compare them: what stays the same?

## Do: extract (35 minutes)

Each team takes one or two plants.

1. Weigh the plant material on the kitchen scale. Record the mass.
2. Put it in a labelled jar with 500 mL of water measured in the jug.
3. The guide stands the jars in a big pot of water on the stove and heats it until the pot water is hot but not boiling (small bubbles on the bottom, about 80–90 °C). Keep it there for **30 minutes**.
4. While you wait, cut ten 6 cm × 6 cm squares of white paper and label one for each extract.
5. The guide lifts the jars out with tongs. When they are cool enough to touch, pour each through a strainer lined with a clean cloth into a second labelled jar. Squeeze out the last drops (gloves on).

What did you just do? You **dissolved** some substances out of the plant (an extraction), then **filtered** out the solid. That is two separation methods in one.

## Notice something puzzling

Look at each extract in a clear cup against white paper. Then put a drop of each on its paper square.

The karkade is deep red in the cup. The onion water is red-brown. The cabbage is purple. But the drops on paper are often a different shade from the liquid in the cup. Why might the colour look different in a thin layer?

## Theory: why colour? (15 minutes)

Read **Dyes and mordants**, the section *Why a dye has colour*. You'll learn:

- that white light is a mixture of colours,
- that a dye absorbs some colours and you see the rest,
- what complementary colours are,
- which molecules colour each of our six plants.

Then look back at **Solutions and concentration**, the section on **extraction**, and **Mixtures and separation** on filtering.

## Do it better: how strong is each dye? (25 minutes)

A strong dye can be diluted many times before its colour disappears. Measure it.

1. Line up eight clear cups on white paper, labelled 1 to 8.
2. Put 20 mL of your extract in cup 1.
3. Put 10 mL of water in cups 2 to 8.
4. Take 10 mL from cup 1 and add it to cup 2. Stir. Take 10 mL from cup 2 into cup 3. Carry on to cup 8.
5. Each cup is **half** the concentration of the one before. Find the last cup where you can still see colour, looking down through the cup at the white paper.

If the last coloured cup is cup $n$, the extract was diluted by $2^{n-1}$ before the colour vanished. Call this its **colour strength**.

| Extract | Mass of plant (g) | Concentration (g/L) | Last coloured cup | Colour strength $2^{n-1}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Work out each concentration in g/L: mass ÷ 0.5 L.

## Record

Copy the class table for all six extracts. Stick the dried paper drops beside it. Answer:

1. Which extract is strongest? Which is weakest?
2. Turmeric looks yellow. Which colours of light is it absorbing? Do the same for karkade.
3. The colour strength only goes up in doubling steps. How could you make the measurement more precise?

Keep your extracts in a cool place with lids on. The guide will make fresh baths for dyeing.
