## Do: rope and slinky waves

Work in threes on a clear stretch of floor.

1. Lay a 5 m rope (or a slinky) straight along the floor. One person holds the far end still.
2. Flick your end sideways once. Watch a single **pulse** run along and bounce back.
3. Now shake it side to side steadily. Shake slowly, then fast. What happens to the length of each wave?
4. With the slinky, push and pull along its length instead of sideways. Watch the coils bunch up and spread out.

**Notice:** does the rope itself travel to the far end? Does a piece of tape stuck to the middle of the rope travel?

## Do: ripples in a tray

Put 1–2 cm of water in a shallow tray. Dip a pencil tip in and out at a steady rate.

Put a ruler beside the tray and a mark on the side.

- **Count** how many ripples pass the mark in 10 seconds. Then find frequency: $f = \text{count} \div 10$.
- **Measure** the distance between two neighbouring crests (the wavelength $\lambda$). It's easier to measure across 5 crests and divide by 5.
- Repeat, dipping faster.

Record it in a table:

| Trial | Ripples in 10 s | $f$ (Hz) | $\lambda$ (cm) | $f \times \lambda$ (cm/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| slow | | | | |
| fast | | | | |

## Puzzle

Look at the last column. When you dipped faster, the frequency went up, and the wavelength went… down. What do you notice about $f \times \lambda$?

That number is the **speed** of the wave. It stays roughly the same, because it depends on the water, not on your pencil.

## Theory: just enough

Read *Waves and vibrations* and *Frequency and period*. You'll meet the wave equation

$$v = f\lambda$$

and use *Rearranging formulas* to get $\lambda = v/f$ or $f = v/\lambda$.

## Do it better

Time the rope: send one pulse and time how long it takes to go 5 m and back. Then $v = 10\ \text{m} \div \text{time}$. Now shake at a counted rate and **predict** the wavelength with $\lambda = v/f$. Measure it. How close were you?

## The question it leaves

Radio waves cross the city with no rope and no water. What are they waves *in*, and how fast do they go? Hold that thought: first we need to see how electricity can make something move.
