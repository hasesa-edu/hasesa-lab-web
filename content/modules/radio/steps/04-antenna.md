## Safety first

Before anyone goes outside, the guide gives the antenna safety briefing. **Never near power lines. Never during a thunderstorm. Ladders only with an adult holding them.**

## Do: string up the antenna

1. **Choose the route.** Find two high points at least 10 m apart (better 20–30 m), such as a tree, a pole or a roof edge. Look up and check there are **no power lines** anywhere nearby.
2. **Tie insulators** (plastic bottle necks or proper insulators) to each end of the antenna wire, then tie nylon rope to the insulators. The wire must not touch the tree, wall or any metal.
3. **Raise it** as high as you safely can. Higher and longer catches more signal.
4. **Run a down-lead** from one end of the antenna to where the radio will sit.
5. **Make a ground.** Clamp a wire onto a metal water pipe (scrape the paint off first), or hammer a metal rod at least 1 m into damp soil. Pour water around it in the dry season.

## Notice

Clip your antenna and ground into the guide's radio in place of the guide's own. Does it still play? Is it louder or quieter?

Try:
- antenna only, no ground;
- ground only, no antenna;
- a short 2 m wire instead of the long one.

**Puzzle:** why does a longer, higher wire work better? And why does the radio need a connection to the earth at all?

## Theory: just enough

Read *The electromagnetic spectrum*. Radio waves are electromagnetic waves, like light. They travel at

$$c = 3.0 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$$

so for any station, $\lambda = c/f$. You'll need *Scientific notation* to handle these numbers.

## The antenna problem

Your guide will tell you the frequency of a station you can receive here (found with an ordinary AM radio). In your notebook:

1. Write the frequency in Hz using scientific notation.
2. Calculate its wavelength $\lambda = c/f$.
3. Calculate a quarter-wavelength.
4. Compare with the length of your antenna. How many times shorter is it?

**Example:** a station on 900 kHz: $\lambda = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{9.0 \times 10^5} \approx 333$ m. A quarter-wave is about 83 m.

## Why a short antenna still works

As the wave passes, its changing electric and magnetic fields push the electrons in **any** wire back and forth at the station's frequency. A longer, higher wire simply catches more of that energy. The current flows down the wire, through your radio and into the ground, and back again, a million times a second. The ground completes the loop.

The antenna catches **every** station at once, all mixed together. Next time: how do we pick just one?
