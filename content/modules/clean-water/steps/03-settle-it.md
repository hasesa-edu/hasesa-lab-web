## Build: a clarity tube (10 minutes)

To compare water fairly, you need a number, not "looks cleaner".

1. Draw a thick black cross on white paper and tape it under a tall clear bottle or tube.
2. Stick a paper ruler up the side, in centimetres, from the bottom.
3. Pour water in slowly while looking down from the top. Stop when you **can't see the cross** any more. Read the depth.

Deeper means clearer. Tap water should go past the top. Record the muddy water's depth.

## Do: the jar test (30 minutes)

Four identical jars, each with 500 mL of well-stirred muddy water:

| Jar | Add |
|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing (the control) |
| 2 | A pinch of alum (about 0.05 g) dissolved in a spoon of water |
| 3 | 2 moringa seed kernels, crushed to powder, mixed with a spoon of water |
| 4 | A bigger pinch of alum (about 0.2 g) |

Stir every jar **fast** for 1 minute, then **slowly** for 5 minutes. Then leave them completely still. Watch for small clumps (**flocs**) forming and sinking.

After 30 minutes, gently pour off water from near the top of each jar and measure its clarity depth.

## Notice something puzzling

In Jar 1, fine clay hardly sank at all, even after half an hour. A pinch of white powder in Jar 2 made it fall in minutes. How can such a small amount make such a big difference?

## Theory: treating water

Read **Treating water**. You'll learn:

- why tiny clay particles repel each other and stay floating,
- how alum and moringa neutralise their charge so they clump into flocs,
- the four barriers: settle, filter, disinfect, store safely,
- why a single barrier is never enough.

## Record

Results table: jar, what was added, clarity depth after 30 minutes. Which coagulant worked best? Was more alum better? Would you choose alum or moringa for a village without shops, and why?
