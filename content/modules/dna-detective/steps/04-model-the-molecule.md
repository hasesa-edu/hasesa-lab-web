## Build a double helix (50 minutes)

Each team builds a model at least 12 base pairs long.

1. **Backbones:** two pipe cleaners or lengths of string, side by side.
2. **Bases:** choose four colours of beads (or sweets) for A, T, G and C, and write your key in your notebook.
3. **Rungs:** on toothpicks, thread one base from each backbone so they meet in the middle. Remember the rule: **A only with T, G only with C**.
4. Fix each rung to both backbones, about 1.5 cm apart.
5. When it's done, hold the top and bottom and **twist** it gently into a right-handed spiral. About 10 rungs make one full turn in real DNA.

Check another team's model: are all the pairs correct?

## Do: the copying machine (15 minutes)

Write a 12-letter sequence for one strand (for example ATGCCGTATAGC). Unzip it: write the two strands separately. Now build the partner for each strand using the pairing rule. Do you get two identical copies of the original?

This is how a cell copies its DNA before it divides.

## Do: secret messages (25 minutes)

Real DNA uses three-letter "words" (codons). Invent a simple code: give each letter of the alphabet a three-base code (for example, AAA = A, AAT = B…). Swap codes with another team.

1. Write a short message (a name or a word) in DNA code.
2. Give the other team only **one strand**, without its partner. Can they work out the other strand?
3. Can they decode the message?

## Record

A photo or drawing of your model with its key. The sequences from the copying exercise. Your coded message and the decoded answer. Then answer: one base is changed by mistake (a **mutation**) in a gene. Why might that matter, and why might it not?
