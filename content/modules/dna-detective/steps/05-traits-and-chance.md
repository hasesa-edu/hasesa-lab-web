## Theory: inheritance

Read **Inheritance**. You'll learn:

- what alleles, genotypes and phenotypes are,
- dominant and recessive alleles,
- how to draw a Punnett square and read off the chances,
- why real results aren't always exactly the predicted ratio,
- why tongue rolling is not a good example of a single gene.

## Do: predict (20 minutes)

In pea plants, purple flowers (**P**) are dominant over white (**p**). Draw Punnett squares to predict the percentage of purple and white offspring for these crosses:

1. PP × pp
2. Pp × Pp
3. Pp × pp

## Do: test with coins (40 minutes)

Each student is a **Pp** parent. Mark one coin face "P" and the other "p" with tape. Pair up: you're the two parents.

1. Each parent tosses a coin. Together, the two results are one baby's genotype.
2. Record 40 babies. Count PP, Pp and pp, and the number of purple and white flowers.
3. Combine the whole class's results.

Then repeat for **Pp × pp** (one parent's coin has "p" on both faces).

## Notice something puzzling

Your pair's 40 babies probably didn't give exactly 30 purple and 10 white. The whole class's results were probably much closer to 3 : 1. Why does adding more results bring you closer to the prediction?

## Do: a family mystery (30 minutes)

Two parents with normal blood both carry one allele for **sickle-cell anaemia** (a recessive condition). Their first child has the condition.

1. What are the parents' genotypes?
2. What is the chance their next child also has it? Is a carrier? Is not a carrier?
3. After three children with the condition, what is the chance the fourth has it?

## Record (30 minutes)

Your Punnett squares, your pair and class coin results, and a bar chart comparing predicted and actual numbers. Answers to the family mystery, with reasons. Explain in two sentences why genetics uses probability.
