## Do: onion cells (25 minutes)

1. Peel the thin, see-through skin from the inside of an onion layer, with tweezers.
2. Lay it flat in a drop of water on a slide (or clear plastic). Add a drop of iodine. Cover with a cover slip or thin plastic, lowering it from one side to avoid bubbles.
3. Look at low power first, then higher.

Draw a few cells. Label the **cell wall**, **cytoplasm** and **nucleus** (the dark dot).

## Do: your own cheek cells (25 minutes)

1. Gently scrape the inside of your cheek with a clean cotton swab or the flat end of a clean toothpick.
2. Smear it on a clean slide in a drop of water. Add a drop of methylene blue (or iodine).
3. Cover and look.

Your cells are flatter, rounder and more scattered than onion cells. Draw a few. What's missing compared with the onion cells? Put your swab and slide straight in the waste bag.

## Theory: cells

Read **Cells**. You'll learn what each part of a cell does, the differences between plant and animal cells, and how to work out real sizes from magnification or from the field of view.

## Do: how big? (25 minutes)

1. At low power, put a clear plastic ruler on the stage and measure the width of the circle you see (the field of view), in mm.
2. Count how many onion cells fit across that circle. Estimate the length of one cell in µm.
3. Do the same for cheek cells at the same power.
4. A DNA double helix is 2 nm wide. Read **Scientific notation**, then write the onion cell length, the cheek cell width and the helix width in metres, in scientific notation.
5. How many times wider is a cheek cell than a DNA helix?

## Notice something puzzling

The nucleus of a cheek cell is about 6 µm across, yet it holds about 2 m of DNA. How can it fit? (Think of the thinness of the helix, and how thread is wound on a spool.)

## Record

Labelled drawings of both cell types, your size calculations with working, and the answer to the puzzle.
