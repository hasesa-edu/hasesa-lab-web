# DNA Detective: guide's notes

For the guide only. About 12 hours, best as six sessions of about two hours, for up to 16 students in teams of four. You handle the alcohol, the hot agar and the battery connections. Everything else, students can do with close supervision.

---

## Before the module

### 1. Practise the extraction twice

Banana and strawberry work reliably; kiwi and onion work but give less. The tricks:

- **Very cold alcohol.** Keep it in the freezer for at least a few hours (it won't freeze). This is the single biggest factor.
- **Gentle handling.** Shaking breaks DNA strands into tiny pieces that won't clump.
- **Slow pouring.** Tilt the tube and run the alcohol down the side so it floats as a layer.
- Clear washing-up liquid without added colour or "with bleach" claims works best.

### 2. Practise the electrophoresis (a week ahead)

**Buffer:** about 1 g of baking soda per litre of distilled or bottled water (a little more makes it run faster but heat more; recipes vary, so test yours).

**Gel:** 1 g of agar powder in 100 mL of buffer. Heat in a microwave or on a hot plate, stirring, until completely clear (it must boil briefly). Cool to about 60 °C (hot to touch but not scalding), then pour about 1 cm deep. Insert the comb immediately. Leave 30 minutes.

**Running:** cover the gel with buffer by 2–3 mm. Four 9 V batteries in series (36 V). Most food colourings are negatively charged and move towards the **positive** electrode, so the wells go at the negative end. Expect 1–3 cm of movement in 30–60 minutes. The batteries will warm a little; disconnect them when not running.

**The case samples:** make each "drink" from food colourings, for example:

| Sample | Mix |
|---|---|
| Crime scene | green colouring + a drop of red |
| Suspect 1 | green only |
| Suspect 2 | green + red (same as crime scene) |
| Suspect 3 | blue + yellow (looks green but separates differently) |
| Suspect 4 | red + blue |

Test it: the crime scene and suspect 2 must give the same pattern, and suspect 3 must look similar by eye but differ in the gel. Change the case if your colourings behave differently. Mix each sample with a few drops of glycerine or a pinch of sugar so it sinks into the well.

### 3. Shopping

| Item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Alcohol | 99% isopropyl or 95% ethanol from a pharmacy. 70% works less well. Store away from heat. |
| Agar | 10 g is plenty. |
| Batteries | Two sets of four 9 V batteries, plus clips and crocodile leads. |
| Electrodes | Stainless-steel wire (from a hardware shop) or pencil leads. Avoid copper, which colours the buffer. |
| Microscope | If there's none, a clip-on phone macro lens shows onion cells; cheek cells are harder. |

## Safety briefing (step 1; again at steps 3 and 6)

- **Alcohol:** you pour; no flames or hot plates while it's out; it is not for drinking.
- Glasses and gloves for extraction and gels.
- **Batteries:** only you connect them. Dry hands. Nobody touches the box while connected. 36 V from batteries is low risk, but the habit matters.
- **Hot agar:** you pour.
- **Cheek cells:** each student uses only their own swab; everything goes straight in the waste bag, which you close and dispose of.
- Nothing is eaten.

## Session plans

### Step 1: The invisible molecule (45 min)

Run the demo slowly so everyone sees the strands rise. Keep the tube for comparison later.

### Step 2: Cells up close (90 min)

Onion cells: about 100–300 µm long. Cheek cells: about 50–60 µm across, nucleus about 5–6 µm. A cheek cell is about 25,000–30,000 times wider than the DNA helix (60 µm ÷ 2 nm = 30,000). Puzzle: DNA is extremely thin and is wound tightly around proteins (like thread on spools), then coiled again and again.

### Step 3: Break it open (120 min)

**Expected class results:**

- No detergent: little or no DNA (the membranes don't break).
- No salt: much less DNA, finer and less clumped.
- No protease: often still works well with fruit; slightly less, or more protein mixed in. Don't overclaim.
- Room-temperature alcohol: noticeably less, slower.

The clump also contains RNA, proteins and some carbohydrates (pectin) from the fruit. It's "DNA-rich", not pure DNA. That's an honest answer to the puzzle.

### Step 4: Model the molecule (90 min)

Check pairs carefully. Mutation question: a changed base may change the protein (possibly harmful, occasionally helpful), or may change nothing at all (some codons code for the same amino acid, and much DNA isn't in genes).

### Step 5: Traits and chance (120 min)

**Predictions:** PP × pp → 100% purple (all Pp). Pp × Pp → 75% purple, 25% white. Pp × pp → 50% each. Pair results of 40 vary a lot (anywhere from about 24–36 purple is normal for Pp × Pp); the class total is usually close to 3 : 1.

**Sickle-cell:** both parents are carriers (Ss). Each child has a 25% chance of the condition, 50% of being a carrier, 25% of being neither, **every time**, regardless of earlier children. Handle this topic with sensitivity; some students may have family members with genetic conditions.

### Step 6: The gel (150 min)

Can be split: pour the gel at the end of one session (cover it with cling film and keep it cool) and run it next time. Students load the wells; it takes practice, so let them practise loading water with a dropper on a spare gel first.

### Step 7: Case closed (105 min)

Debate: keep it respectful and evidence-based. There's no single right answer. Sign-off conversations happen during the report writing.

## Judging the sign-off items

- **extraction:** visible DNA; correct roles for each step.
- **control-step:** compared with a full-method control; result explained.
- **model:** correct pairs; writes the complementary strand quickly.
- **punnett:** correct squares; explains that larger samples get closer to the ratio.
- **gel:** identifies suspect 2 (or your equivalent); explains separation by charge and size.
- **ethics:** a real benefit and a real risk, each with a reason.
