## Safety first

Glasses on for every breaking test. The bucket hangs no more than 10 cm above the floor, over a box, so it can't fall far. Keep your feet and hands out from under it.

## Measure: shrink and crack (25 minutes)

For each of your bars:

1. Measure the distance between the two toothpick holes, in mm, as carefully as you can. It was 100 mm when wet, so the **shrinkage** is simply $100 - \text{new distance}$, in percent.
2. Give it a **crack score**:
   - 0: no cracks you can see,
   - 1: thin hairline cracks,
   - 2: cracks you can slide a piece of paper into,
   - 3: cracked right across, curled or broken.
3. Weigh the bar you weighed wet. How much mass did it lose? That was water.

$$\text{water lost (\%)} = \frac{\text{wet mass} - \text{dry mass}}{\text{wet mass}} \times 100$$

## Theory: structures and forces

Read **Structures and forces**, the parts on *Tension and compression*. You'll learn:

- that a wall carrying a roof is in **compression** (squashed),
- that a bent bar is squashed on top and **stretched** (in tension) underneath,
- why brick, stone and mud are strong when squashed but weak when stretched.

## Do: the bending test (35 minutes)

Build the tester:

1. Put two bricks on the floor. Lay a round pencil on each, exactly **10 cm** apart.
2. Lay a dry bar across the pencils, flat side down.
3. Tie a loop of string around the middle of the bar and hang the empty bucket from it, over a box. The bucket should hang just above the box.
4. Add sand **one cup at a time**, gently, waiting five seconds between cups.
5. When the bar snaps, stop. Weigh the bucket with its sand and string. That is the **breaking load**.

Test all three bars of each of your mixes. Before you start, predict which mix will hold the most.

## Do: the crushing test (30 minutes)

Now squash a small brick, the way a wall's weight squashes the bricks at the bottom.

1. Measure the **end** of a matchbox brick: width and thickness in cm. Area = width × thickness, in cm².
2. Put a tile on a bathroom scale, and stand the brick **on its end** in the middle. Put a second tile on top.
3. One person presses down on the top tile with both hands, **slowly and steadily**, harder and harder. A second person films the scale with a phone.
4. Stop as soon as the brick crushes. Play the video back and find the highest reading. That is the crushing load, in kg.

Crush three bricks of each mix. Keep the last two bricks for the drip test.

## Do it better: from kilograms to pressure

A mass of 1 kg weighs about 10 N. So a crushing load of 50 kg is a force of about 500 N.

The pressure on the brick when it crushed is

$$P = \frac{F}{A}$$

**Example.** 50 kg = 500 N, on an end of 3.2 cm × 1.4 cm = 4.5 cm² = 0.00045 m². $P = 500 \div 0.00045 \approx 1{,}100{,}000$ Pa, about 1.1 MPa.

Work out the crushing pressure for your best brick. Then think: the bottom of a solid mud wall 2.5 m tall is squashed by a pressure of about 40 kPa (0.04 MPa) from its own weight. How many times stronger than that is your brick?

## Record

Copy all your results onto the class board. For each mix, work out the **mean** and **range** of shrinkage, breaking load and crushing load.
