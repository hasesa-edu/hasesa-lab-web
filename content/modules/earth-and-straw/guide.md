# Earth & Straw: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 10 hours, run as seven sessions of 60–105 minutes, for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. The mud needs drying time, so the timetable matters more than in most modules:

| Session | Step | Minutes | Gap before the next session |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Two bars of mud | 60 | any (students collect soil) |
| 2 | What's in the ground? | 90 | **at least 1 day** (jar tests settle) |
| 3 | Clay glue | 75 | **at least 4 days** (sausages dry) |
| 4 | Make your bricks | 100 | **12–14 days** (bricks dry) |
| 5 | Shrink, crack, break | 105 | any |
| 6 | Rain on the wall | 75 | any |
| 7 | Build it, back it | 105 | panels dry a week before the final check |

During the long gap after step 4, students can re-read *Soils and clay*, *Structures and forces* and *Pressure*. In the kiremt rains (June to September) drying takes longer; dry the bricks indoors on shelves and allow three weeks. Two weeks is enough in the dry season (bega).

Everything is done outdoors or on plastic sheets. Mud gets everywhere: have buckets of water, rags and soap ready at every session.

## Before the module

### 1. Find the building soil (three weeks ahead)

You need about 20 kg of one clay-rich subsoil that the whole class uses for the fair test. Dig it from below the dark topsoil (30 cm or more) at a building site or a field, with permission. Do your own ribbon test: you want a ribbon of 3–6 cm (roughly 25–45% clay). That leaves room for sand to improve it.

- **Red highland soils** (nitisols, common in and around Addis Ababa) are ideal.
- **Black cotton soil** (vertisol, the dark soil that opens wide cracks in the dry season, also very common around Addis) contains a strongly swelling clay. Pure, it shrinks 12–20% and cracks badly. It still works for the test, and it makes the sand and straw effects dramatic, but expect mix A to be wrecked and mix C to be best. If you can, have a bag of each so students can compare in step 3.

Dry the soil in the sun, break up the lumps with a stick, and pick out stones and roots. Store it in sacks.

### 2. Make the two hook bars (two to three weeks ahead)

From the building soil make two bars in carton moulds (see below):

- **Bar 1:** soil only, mixed wet and soft (a bit too much water makes it more dramatic).
- **Bar 2:** 2 cups soil, 2 cups sand, 1 cup chopped straw (mix D).

Press two toothpick holes 100 mm apart in each. Dry them in the sun (bar 1) and the shade (bar 2) to exaggerate the difference. Bar 1 should end up curled, cracked and shorter; bar 2 smooth and sound. Make spares: they break.

### 3. Moulds

- **Bar moulds** (18 needed for the class, plus spares): cut a 1 L juice carton in half lengthwise to make a trough. Cut a strip of card from another carton, fold it into a U-shaped channel 4 cm wide and 2 cm deep, about 15 cm long, tape the ends closed with card, and stand it inside the trough so it keeps its shape. Line it with plastic bag. Cartons are waxed, so mud doesn't soak them.
- **Matchbox moulds:** the inner trays, about 5 × 3.5 × 1.5 cm. Line each with a scrap of plastic. You need 30 plus spares.

### 4. Testing apparatus

- **Bending tester:** two bricks, two round pencils or dowels, string, a 5–10 L bucket with a handle, a box or crate under the bucket, a small sack of dry sand and a cup. A kitchen scale to 5 kg (or the bathroom scale) to weigh bucket and sand after each break.
- **Crushing test:** a bathroom scale (mechanical or digital, to at least 120 kg), two tiles or small boards, a phone to film the dial. Check the scale reads zero with the bottom tile on it, or tell students to subtract the tile.
- **Drip test:** for each station, a 500 mL bottle with a pinhole in the cap (heat a thin nail and push it through, you do this), held upside down in a stand with the pinhole 40 cm above the brick. A stack of bricks, a chair with the bottle taped to the back, or a stick in a bucket of sand all work. Test the drip rate: 100 mL should take roughly 20–40 minutes. Widen the hole if it is slower. Set up six stations.
- **Chika panel frames:** one per team, 40 × 40 cm, from sticks about 2 cm thick nailed at the corners, with a diagonal brace across one corner. Have bundles of thin sticks (about 1 cm) cut to 40 cm for the mager, twine, and small nails.

### 5. Shopping and collecting

- Matchboxes and cartons: ask students and staff to save them from a month ahead.
- Sand: one or two sacks from a construction-materials seller. Sieve out stones.
- Teff straw (chid): grain and feed sellers, or ask at a farm on the edge of the city. A small sack is plenty.
- Ash: cold ash from a charcoal or wood stove, sieved through a kitchen sieve outdoors (wear a mask).
- Dung: fresh cow dung from a cattle keeper, collected on the day of step 4 (and step 7) in a bucket with a lid. It is optional; ash alone is fine.
- Detergent powder, gloves, masks and glasses: shops and pharmacies.

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1, repeat at every session)

- Gloves on for soil, ash and dung. Hands washed with soap before eating and at the end. Nobody eats or drinks in the mud area.
- Anyone with a cut on their hands does not touch dung; give them the measuring and recording jobs.
- Ash is alkaline: glasses and gloves when mixing it, no throwing or blowing.
- Dry soil and ash are mixed outdoors and dampened quickly. Masks for sieving.
- Students' soil comes only from places you approve. Reject any sample with glass, rubbish, or a bad smell (it may be from a latrine or drain area).
- At the bending tester: glasses on, the bucket hangs over a box no more than 10 cm below, nobody's feet underneath. Add sand gently.
- At the crushing test: press slowly; glasses on, because fragments can fly sideways.
- Heavy sacks: two people. You do all cutting and nailing, or stand right beside anyone who does.

## Session plans

### Step 1: Two bars of mud (60 min)

Hand round the bars only after the rules. Let students measure the gap between the holes in each: bar 1 will be around 88–92 mm, bar 2 around 95–97 mm. Don't tell them what's in either bar. Good guesses: "straw", "sand", "less water", "dried in shade". All are partly right, which is what makes the fair test necessary.

If you can bring a piece of old chika wall (from a demolition site), it shows the straw clearly. Otherwise use a photo.

The homework soil is important: without it, step 2 is less personal. Bring a few spare samples (red and black soils) for students who forget.

### Step 2: What's in the ground? (90 min)

The ribbon test takes practice. Show it yourself first with the building soil and with sand. Common faults: too much water (sloppy, makes everything look like clay) or too little (cracks immediately). It should feel like putty.

Jar tests: the detergent matters; without it clay clumps and settles too fast, mixing into the silt layer. Make sure lids are tight before shaking. Mark the jars with names and keep them somewhere nobody bumps. You may want to make one jar of the building soil yourself, so there is a reliable one.

**Sieve question:** a kitchen sieve holds back coarse sand and stones; silt and clay are both far too small to be stopped by any kitchen sieve, which is why we let them settle instead.

### Step 3: Clay glue (75 min)

**Expected jar results.** Red subsoil: often 30–45% sand, 20–30% silt, 25–40% clay. Black cotton soil: 10–25% sand, 20–30% silt, 45–65% clay. Garden topsoil: more silt and sand, 10–25% clay. Clay layers are hard to see when the water is still cloudy; if needed, read after 2–3 days. Accept answers within about 10 percentage points of the ribbon estimate as "close"; the jar test is rough because the layers blur together and clay settles loosely, which tends to overestimate it.

The tile demonstration works best with very smooth tiles or two flat pieces of plastic or glass. Pulled straight apart, wet tiles resist; slid sideways, they move easily. This is how clay plates behave: stuck together, but able to slide, which is why wet clay is plastic.

**Sausages.** Typical shrinkage after 4–5 days in shade: soil only 6–10% (more for black soil), 1:1 3–5%, 1:2 1–3%. The 1:2 sausage may crumble when pressed if the soil isn't clay-rich enough. Keep them on a flat board in shade; label the board, not the sausage.

### Step 4: Make your bricks (100 min)

**Fair-test answers.** Sand: compare A, B, C. Straw: compare B, D, E. Stabiliser: compare D and F. Controlled variables: the same soil, same sand, same cup and way of filling it, same mould size, same packing method, same drying place and time, same consistency (drop test), same testing method. Water can't be held constant, because sandy mixes need less water to reach the same stiffness; holding stiffness constant is the fairer choice. Students should record the water anyway.

Organise teams so all six mixes are made: with four teams, two teams take two mixes each. Each mix needs 3 bars and 5 matchbox bricks. A 4-cup dry batch gives just enough; make a little extra.

**Where students get stuck.** Too much water (mud slumps out of the mould, shrinks more and makes the test unfair): the drop test catches this. Air pockets: they must throw the mud in hard and press it into corners. Straw not chopped: long straw won't fit in a matchbox. Labels: insist on mix letter and number on every piece.

**Drying.** After 2 days, slide the bars and bricks out of the moulds on their plastic, peel the plastic, and turn them over. Then leave them in shade, out of wind, on a flat shelf. Weigh the wet bars to the nearest 5 g. A wet bar of about 120 cm³ weighs roughly 230–260 g.

### Step 5: Shrink, crack, break (105 min)

Run the bending and crushing tests as stations, teams rotating. The bending test is slow (one cup at a time); it is fine to add sand in half-cups near the end if a team is clearly far from breaking.

**Expected results** (red subsoil of about 35% clay; your numbers will differ, the pattern should not):

| Mix | Shrinkage (%) | Crack score | Breaking load (kg) | Crushing load (kg) | Water lost (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (soil only) | 7–12 | 2–3 | 1–5, very variable | 40–90 if uncracked | 20–25 |
| B (1:1) | 3–6 | 0–1 | 2–4 | 30–60 | 15–18 |
| C (1:2) | 1–3 | 0 | 0.8–2 | 15–35 | 12–15 |
| D (1:1 + 1 straw) | 3–5 | 0–1 | 2.5–5 | 30–55 | 16–20 |
| E (1:1 + 2 straw) | 2.5–4 | 0 | 2–4 | 20–45 | 17–22 |
| F (D + ash or dung) | 3–5 | 0–1 | 2.5–5 | 30–60 | 16–20 |

Key patterns: more sand, less shrinkage and cracking but weaker; pure soil is strong where it isn't cracked, so its results are all over the place (a wide range is itself a finding); some straw helps (bars with straw bend and hold together after the first crack instead of snapping cleanly); too much straw weakens the brick because there is less mud to carry the load.

Bending: cracks start at the **bottom** of the bar (the stretched side). Students who see this have understood tension.

**Crushing pressure check.** 50 kg ≈ 500 N, on about 4.5 cm² (0.00045 m²), gives about 1.1 MPa. Adobe typically crushes at 0.5–2 MPa. A mud wall 2.5 m tall with a density of about 1,700 kg/m³ puts 1,700 × 10 × 2.5 ≈ 42,000 Pa (about 0.04 MPa) on its base, so even a weak brick is about 10–50 times stronger than needed. Walls fail from cracks, water and sideways forces, not from being crushed. This is a good discussion point.

Mechanical bathroom scales lag and overshoot; the video helps. Accept readings to the nearest 5 kg.

**Optional for strong students** (not needed for sign-off): the bending stress at breaking is $\sigma = \dfrac{3FL}{2bd^2}$, with L = span, b = width, d = depth. For 3 kg (30 N), L = 0.10 m, b = 0.038 m, d = 0.019 m: σ = 9 ÷ (2 × 0.038 × 0.000361) ≈ 330,000 Pa ≈ 0.33 MPa.

### Step 6: Rain on the wall (75 min)

Set up all six drip stations first, then run the dunk test and graph work while they drip.

**Expected pit depths** (100 mL from 40 cm): A 1–4 mm (if uncracked), B 2–6 mm, C 6–15 mm (sandy mixes erode fast), D 2–6 mm, E 3–8 mm, F with dung 0–3 mm, F with ash 1–5 mm. In the dunk test, C slumps within a minute or two; A and B go cloudy and slowly soften; dung-stabilised pieces keep their shape longest. No mix is waterproof: that is why real chika walls rely on roof overhangs, stone footings and fresh plaster after the rains.

**Physical or chemical?** Softening in water is a physical property: plain dried mud is still the same clay, sand and water. A good answer about F: dung and ash seem to make the mud resist water in a way drying alone does not, so something more may be happening (ash and lime react with clay; dung adds sticky substances and fibres). Accept "it might be a chemical change" with a reason.

**Graphs.** Sand graph: shrinkage falls as sand rises, steeply at first then flattening. Straw graph: often a rise from 0 to 1 cup then a level or fall at 2 cups: a peak. Bar chart: C clearly worst. Students often join dots for three points; ask for a best-fit line or smooth curve, and ask whether three points are enough to be sure of the shape.

### Step 7: Build it, back it (105 min)

The usual winner is D or F (1:1 with some straw, with dung for an outside wall). With black cotton soil, expect C or a 1:2 mix with straw to win. Any mix is acceptable if the evidence supports it and the weakness is honest.

Panels: the mud must be thrown hard and pushed through from both sides so it locks around the mager. Thinner than 2.5 cm cracks; thicker than 4 cm sags. Mist cracked panels with water after a day and press the cracks closed with a wet thumb, as builders do. Check panels a week later for cracks and for whether the mud still grips the sticks.

**Questions to ask during the explanation:**

- If clay is the glue, why not use only clay?
- Your crushing test says a brick is 20 times stronger than it needs to be. So why do mud houses fall down?
- Why do builders leave chika to soak and tread it for days before plastering?
- How would you protect your wall from the kiremt rains without cement?

## Judging the sign-off items

- **soil-test:** the ribbon length is recorded, the jar layers were measured in mm, and the percentages are calculated correctly and add up to about 100%.
- **explains-clay:** must say that clay particles are tiny (flat) and are held together by water between them, and that when the water leaves the clay shrinks, which cracks the brick. "Clay is sticky" alone isn't enough.
- **fair-test:** the student can say which mixes compare sand and which compare straw, and their bricks were really made to the recipe (check the water record and labels). Three bars per mix tested.
- **measures:** the table has all four kinds of measurement with means and ranges, and the shrinkage working is right.
- **graph:** correct axes and units, a best-fit line or curve (not dot-to-dot), and a sentence describing the trend. A bar chart counts for the drip test, but at least one line graph is needed.
- **recommends:** three real numbers from their data, one honest weakness with a sensible fix. A recommendation that ignores clearly contrary data (for example choosing C despite its pits) without a reason doesn't pass.
- **panel:** mud fills the frame on both sides, grips the sticks, and doesn't fall out when the frame is stood up. Small drying cracks are fine.
- **safety:** discuss any repeated lapses with the student before signing.

## Notes for experts reviewing this draft

- The drip test is a scaled-down version of the Geelong drip test for earth blocks; check whether the 40 cm / 100 mL settings and the pit depths above are sensible on matchbox-sized bricks.
- Expected strengths and shrinkages are typical published ranges for adobe; they should be replaced with real classroom data after the first run.
- Confirm dung handling advice with a health professional, and whether lime (nora) could safely be added as a guide-only demonstration.
- The `soils` concept's curriculum tag (chemistry, grade 9) needs confirming; soil also appears in general science and agriculture units.
