# Biogas from Dung: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 10 hours of contact time, spread over about three weeks because the digesters need time. It suits up to 16 students in four or five teams of three or four. Every digester lives outdoors, so you need a sunny, fenced or guarded corner of the compound where bottles won't be knocked over, taken or played with, and a shady spot nearby.

---

## Session plan

| Session | When | Step | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Day −1 (your jerrycan started on day −8) | 1 The gas from nowhere | 60 min |
| 2 | Day 0 | 2 Build your digesters | 105 min |
| — | Every day, days 1–14 | 4 Daily readings (one student per team) | 5 min a day |
| 3 | Day 3 or 4 | 3 The hidden workers | 75 min |
| 4 | Day 7 or 8 | 4 Graphing (the 40-minute part) | 40 min |
| 5 | Day 10–12 | 5 What's in the gas? | 90 min |
| 6 | Day 14 | 6 Bio-slurry (stop digesters, start seed test) | 75 min |
| 7 | Day 19–21 | 7 Advise a household (seeds have had 5–7 days) | 105 min |

Steps 1 and 2 can run in one long afternoon with a break if you can't meet on two days in a row. Start the digesters as early as you can: every extra day of gas helps. If the readings can't be done at weekends, that's fine; students record the day number honestly and the graph has gaps.

Check the weather. Addis Ababa and the highlands are cool, especially at night and in the rainy season (June–September). Shaded bottles there may make almost nothing in two weeks. That is a real result, but make sure the warm box really is warm (see below), or the whole class sees very little gas. The dry season (October–February) with sunny days is best.

## Before the module

### 1. Your demonstration digester (8 days ahead)

Fill a 5 L jerrycan about three-quarters full with fresh cow dung and water, 1 : 1 by mass (about 1.8 kg of each), stirred smooth. Stretch a good balloon several times, squeeze the air out, and tape it tightly over the jerrycan's mouth. Stand it in your warm box in the sun. Check it daily. Squeeze gently: if the slurry is crusting, swirl the jerrycan to break the crust.

By day 7–10 a warm jerrycan usually fills a balloon to 20–40 cm circumference, sometimes more. The first gas is mostly carbon dioxide and won't burn. If the balloon fills fast in the first two or three days, let that early gas out (outdoors, away from flames) and let it refill: the second fill has more methane. If the balloon gets bigger than about 60 cm round, let some gas out.

A spare second jerrycan started a few days after the first is good insurance for the flame test in step 5.

### 2. The warm box

A cardboard box lined with straw or old cloth, bottles wrapped in black plastic bags standing inside, and a sheet of clear plastic taped over the top (a mini greenhouse). Face it to the sun. On a sunny day in Addis it reaches 30–40 °C in the afternoon and holds some heat into the evening. Check it on the first afternoon: if it goes above 45 °C, open the plastic a little. Put a thermometer inside.

### 3. Shopping and preparation

| Item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Fresh cow dung, about 5 kg | Urban dairies in and around the city, or a farmer. Collect it the day before into a bucket with a lid; it must be fresh and moist, not dried. Keep it cool and covered overnight. |
| Bottles | 1.5–2 L, with caps. Fizzy-drink bottles are strongest. Per team: 3 digesters + 3 collectors, plus spares. Two more for your water-only control. |
| Tube | Clear PVC tube, 5–8 mm inside, from hardware or plumbing shops, sold by the metre. About 1 m per digester. Check it fits snugly through the cap hole you make. |
| Caps | Make the hole yourself: heat a nail of the same diameter as the tube over a flame (held in pliers) and push it through the cap, or drill it. Students don't do this. Push the tube 2 cm through, seal both sides with epoxy or modelling clay, and tape over the top. Do this before session 2; epoxy needs a few hours. |
| Balloons | Good-quality round latex balloons. Cheap thin ones leak methane through the rubber over days. Test a few by inflating and leaving them overnight. |
| Limewater | Stir a heaped teaspoon of builders' lime (calcium hydroxide, ኖራ, nora) into 1 L of water in a bottle, shake, let it settle overnight, and pour off the clear liquid. Keep it capped (air slowly turns it milky). Label it. It irritates eyes and skin: gloves and glasses. |
| Feto (garden cress) seeds | Spice sellers in any market. Test a few on wet tissue a week before: germination should be above 80% in two days. |
| Thermometers | Room or kitchen thermometers. A fever thermometer only reads 32–42 °C and is no use. |
| Gloves, soap, plasters | Plenty. Gloves for every session that touches dung or slurry. |

Bottles, balloons and feto are cheap; the dung is usually free if you ask. The thermometers and the kitchen scale are the biggest costs; borrow them if the school has them.

### 4. Disposal pit

Before session 6, dig a hole about 40 cm deep in a garden or compost area, well away from any well, spring, stream or drain. The slurry goes in there and is covered with soil.

## Safety briefing (step 1; repeat the gas and flame rules in step 5)

- **Hygiene.** Dung and slurry carry gut bacteria (including *E. coli* and sometimes *Salmonella*) and worm eggs. Digestion in two weeks at these temperatures does not make it safe. Gloves on, cuts covered, no eating or drinking, no touching faces or phones, handwashing with soap after every session and every daily reading. Students with open wounds on their hands do not handle slurry.
- **Pressure.** A bottle sealed without an outlet can bulge and burst its cap off, spraying slurry. Every digester has a balloon or a tube to a water collector. Water collectors are open to the air through the water, so pressure can't build up. Balloons are limited by their size: let gas out before they get bigger than a head.
- **Flammable gas.** Methane mixed with air in the right proportions can burn fast. Digesters stay outdoors. No flames anywhere near them. Students never light gas. Never light gas inside a bottle, a jerrycan or a balloon, or at the mouth of one.
- **Hydrogen sulfide.** Biogas contains a trace of it (rotten-egg smell). In the tiny amounts from bottles, outdoors, it is not a danger, but nobody puts their nose to a tube and breathes in deeply. Sniff by wafting.
- **Limewater.** Glasses on. Blow, never suck. Wash off splashes with plenty of water.
- **Opening digesters.** Outdoors, slowly, cap pointing away from people.
- **Disposal.** Slurry into the pit, never down a sink or drain. Rinse bottles outdoors and throw them away; don't reuse them for drinking water.

## Step-by-step notes and answer key

### Step 1: The gas from nowhere (60 min)

Do the rules before anyone goes outside. At the jerrycan, let a tiny puff of gas out by easing the balloon neck: the smell is the hook. Measure the balloon's circumference for them.

**The flame test in step 1** is optional. Do it only if the jerrycan has made a lot of gas and you have already checked that it lights (see step 5 notes). Otherwise, say "you'll see in step 5" and keep the mystery.

Common guesses: "the dung is rotting" (close: good), "it's air", "it's steam", "it's the water". Don't correct yet. Collect the "litres from 1 kg" guesses; most will be far too high.

### Step 2: Build your digesters (105 min)

**Assign tests.** With four teams: one food, one temperature, two dilution (or two food), so the class has four standard bottles. With five teams, double up whichever you like. With three teams, one each, and make an extra standard bottle yourself.

**Dilution maths:** for 1 : 2, dung = 1400 ÷ 3 ≈ 467 g and water ≈ 933 g. For 1 : 0.5, dung = 1400 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 933 g and water ≈ 467 g. For 1 : 1, 700 g each.

**Food scraps bottle:** 700 g of mashed scraps plus 700 g water. Dung + scraps: 350 g of each plus 700 g water.

**Controlled variables** students should list: total mass of slurry (1.4 kg), bottle size and type, fill height, freshness and source of dung, gas-collection method, location and temperature (except in the temperature test), the time of reading, the seal.

**Why the water-only control?** Temperature changes make the air in the bottle and collector expand and shrink, so readings move a little even with no gas. The control shows how big that error is.

**Common problems:** leaks round the tube (seal with clay, then tape over it); collector bottles with an air gap at the top before day 0 (note the starting reading rather than starting again); tube slipping out of the collector (tape the tube to the stick). Make sure every team does the "squeeze test" before leaving.

### Step 3: The hidden workers (75 min)

**Expected readings on day 3–4:** standard bottles 0–300 mL; warm food-scrap bottles often the highest so far (fast acid-phase carbon dioxide), smelling sour; shade bottles near zero; control within ±20 mL.

**Maths answers.**

1. Left: 6 C, 12 H, 6 O. Right: 3 C + 3 C = 6 C; 3 × 4 = 12 H; 3 × 2 = 6 O. Balanced.
2. 1 mol glucose gives 3 + 3 = 6 mol gas = 6 × 24 = 144 L. Per gram: 144 ÷ 180 = 0.8 L/g.
3. Half: 50%. Real biogas is richer in methane because carbon dioxide dissolves in the water of the slurry, and because proteins and fats give more methane per gram than sugar.
4. 700 × 0.20 = 140 g solids; 140 × 0.80 = 112 g organic matter.
5. 2 L ÷ 0.8 L/g = 2.5 g. 2.5 ÷ 112 ≈ 0.022, about 2%. So two weeks in a cool bottle digests only a tiny fraction. That's why household plants keep slurry inside for about 50–60 days and are fed every day.

(Gas volumes are bigger in Addis than at sea level because the air pressure is lower, about 25% bigger for the same amount of gas. You can mention this to strong students; it doesn't change the conclusions.)

**Where students get stuck:** they think the methane makers are bacteria. Archaea look like bacteria but are a separate group of living things. They also think "anaerobic" means "dead" or "rotting": stress that the microbes are alive and respiring.

**Questions to ask:** Why do cows burp methane? Where would you expect methane makers in nature? (Swamps, rice fields, rubbish dumps, termite guts, the rumen.) Why does the food-scrap bottle stop?

### Step 4: The daily gas log (5 min a day + 40 min graph)

Make a rota in session 2 so each team knows who reads each day. Check the logs every few days for invented-looking numbers (perfectly regular readings).

**Balloon check:** C = 40 cm: V = 64,000 ÷ (6 × 9.87) = 64,000 ÷ 59.2 ≈ 1,081 cm³. Balloons are only roughly spherical and their rubber squeezes the gas a little, so balloon volumes are good to about ±20%. That's fine for comparisons, as long as every bottle in a test uses the same method.

**Expected results over 14 days in Addis (2 L bottles, 1.4 kg slurry):**

| Bottle | Lag | Total by day 14 | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard (dung 1 : 1, warm box) | 2–4 days | 1–4 L | Steady rise after the lag; fastest rate perhaps 200–400 mL/day. |
| Dung 1 : 1, shade outdoors | 5–10 days | 0–0.8 L | Often almost nothing. |
| Dung 1 : 1, indoors | 3–6 days | 0.3–1.5 L | Between the other two. |
| Food scraps only | none | 0.5–2 L | Fast for 2–4 days, then stops; very sour smell. Gas mostly CO₂, won't burn. |
| Dung + scraps | 1–3 days | 1–5 L | Often the most gas, but can stall like the scraps bottle if too much food. |
| Dung 1 : 0.5 (thick) | 3–5 days | 0.5–3 L | Crust forms, gas gets trapped; often less than 1 : 1 despite more dung. |
| Dung 1 : 2 (thin) | 2–4 days | 0.7–3 L | Less dung, so usually less gas per bottle, but similar or more per kg of dung. |
| Water only | — | within ±30 mL | Up on sunny afternoons, down at night. |

Your class's numbers may be lower or higher; what matters is whether students can read their own graph honestly. The variation between standard bottles is usually large (a factor of two is common), so many differences will not be "sure". Praise students who say so.

**A good extension** for the dilution team: divide total gas by the mass of dung in each bottle to get litres per kg of dung. The thin bottle often wins on this measure even if it loses on total gas, which is the right question for a farmer with limited dung but enough water (and the wrong one for a farmer short of water).

**Where students get stuck:** plotting "gas today" instead of total gas (both are useful, but the curve shape is clearer on total gas); joining dots; taking the gradient between two data points across the lag instead of along the steep part.

### Step 5: What's in the gas? (90 min)

**Getting gas samples.** From a water collector: push a short piece of tube up into the collector from below, under the water, so its end is in the gas at the top; fit a syringe to the other end above the water, and draw 40 mL. From your balloon: pinch the neck, slide a short tube into it, tape round, and draw gas with a syringe while pinching. Keep the samples small; students need only 40 mL each.

**Expected limewater results:** air, no visible change in a minute (it would take far more air); breath, milky in 10–30 seconds; biogas, milky quickly, often faster than breath because biogas has 30–50% CO₂ against about 4% in breath. A bottle that has made only a little gas may give a weaker result because the sample is mostly the air that was in the bottle at the start: ask students why.

**What the test proves and doesn't:** it shows carbon dioxide is present. It doesn't show what else is there, or how much methane. That needs the flame test.

**Flame test (you only).** Do it only if you're confident, outdoors, out of the wind, in shade so the pale blue flame is visible.

1. Use gas that has been collected for at least a week after any early CO₂ was let out. Test it privately the day before with a small puff.
2. Pinch the balloon neck and transfer it from the jerrycan onto one end of a 50 cm–1 m tube, taped tight. Keep the balloon pinched until you're ready.
3. Students 3 m back, with glasses. Bucket of water beside you.
4. Hold the far end of the tube pointing up and away from you, ideally fitted with a narrow nozzle (an empty ballpoint pen tube). Light a long lighter, hold it beside the nozzle, then release the pinch and squeeze the balloon gently.
5. The flame is small, pale blue, sometimes almost invisible in daylight. Squeezing harder makes it longer. If it doesn't light within a few seconds, stop squeezing, take the flame away, and explain why.
6. Never light gas at the balloon or the jerrycan, never with the tube pointed at anyone, and never let students hold anything.

If the gas won't burn, the likely reason is too much carbon dioxide (gas from the early acid phase) or too little gas. Show the step's video instead and discuss.

**Energy maths answers.**

1. 0.6 × 36 = 21.6 MJ/m³, which is 21.6 kJ per litre (1 m³ = 1000 L).
2. Q = 2 × 4200 × (92 − 20) = 2 × 4200 × 72 = 604,800 J ≈ 605 kJ.
3. Useful energy per litre = 0.55 × 21.6 ≈ 11.9 kJ. Litres needed = 605 ÷ 11.9 ≈ 51 L.
4. Example: 2 L ÷ 51 L ≈ 0.04 pots, about 4% of one pot of tea. The bottles prove the science, not the cooking.
5. 1.6 ÷ 0.4 = 4 hours of cooking a day.

### Step 6: Bio-slurry (75 min)

Open digesters one at a time with you beside them. Typical smells: the best gas-makers smell mild and earthy (with a hint of rotten egg); food-scrap bottles smell strongly sour or vinegary; shaded bottles still smell like fresh dung. Slurry from good bottles is darker and smoother than fresh dung.

**Dilutions:** 1 : 4 is 5 mL slurry + 20 mL water; 1 : 1 is 10 mL + 10 mL (make 20 mL and use 10). Strain lumps out so the tissue isn't smothered.

Controlled variables: number of seeds, volume of liquid (10 mL), type of tissue, cup size, place, temperature, light, days before counting, seed batch.

Empty and bury the slurry the same session.

### Step 7: Advise a household (105 min)

**Expected seed results** (they vary with the slurry):

| Liquid | Germination by day 2 | Mean root on day 5 | GI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 85–95% | 20–30 mm | 100 |
| Slurry 1 : 4 | 85–95% | similar or a little longer | about 90–130 |
| Slurry 1 : 1 | 60–90% | shorter | about 50–90 |
| Undiluted slurry | 20–70% | much shorter | about 10–50 |
| Fresh dung 1 : 4 | 60–90% | shorter than digested 1 : 4 | about 50–90 |

The lesson: strong slurry harms germination (ammonia and salts), diluted slurry is fine or helpful. Bio-slurry's real benefit shows over weeks of growth, as nutrients feed the plant; a seed test only shows whether it's safe for seeds. Good students will say this. Farmers usually spread slurry on the soil, dried or composted, or diluted, before planting, not poured onto germinating seeds.

**GI worked example:** water 90% and 25 mm; slurry 1 : 4 at 90% and 28 mm. GI = (90 × 28) ÷ (90 × 25) × 100 = 112.

**"Litres from 1 kg" answer:** e.g. 2 L ÷ 0.7 kg ≈ 2.9 L/kg in two weeks, much less than most students' step 1 guesses and about a tenth of a household plant's yield.

**Recommendation answers.**

- Feed: fresh dung, every day; small amounts of kitchen scraps are fine mixed in, but not lots of scraps alone (they sour the digester). No plastic, bones, sand or soil.
- Water: about 1 : 1, so about 30 L of water a day for 30 kg of dung. Much thicker and it crusts and blocks; much thinner and it needs more water (a real problem in dry areas) and washes food out before it's digested.
- Warmth: underground domes are insulated by the soil and stay at a steadier temperature; in cold places like Debre Birhan, expect less gas in the cold months.
- Gas: 30 × 35 = 1,050 L ≈ 1.05 m³ a day. At 0.4 m³/h that's about 2.6 hours of cooking; at 51 L a pot, about 20 pots of water boiled.
- Slurry: dilute it or let it dry or compost before use; don't pour it neat on seedlings.
- Safety: never use a flame to look for leaks (use soapy water); keep the gas pipe and stove where gas can escape; keep children away from the inlet and outlet pits; wash hands after handling slurry.

Accept sensible answers with numbers and working.

## Questions to ask throughout

- Why can't you see any of the workers? How do you know they're there?
- Where did the energy in the gas come from originally? (The sun, through the grass the cow ate.)
- Is biogas "renewable"? Is burning it better for the climate than letting dung rot in a heap? (Methane is a much stronger greenhouse gas than CO₂; burning it turns it into CO₂.)
- Why does a family with only one cow struggle to run a digester?

## Judging the sign-off items

- **digester:** a sealed digester with a working outlet, and a log with at least 10 dated readings and temperatures. Gaps for weekends are fine; invented numbers are not.
- **fair-test:** the plan names one independent variable, the standard bottle and the water-only control, and lists at least five controlled variables that were actually kept the same (check the masses and fill heights).
- **gas-graph:** total gas against day, axes labelled with units, smooth curves (not dot-to-dot), a key, and a gradient worked out along the steepest part in mL/day.
- **explains-digestion:** names the three stages (or equivalent words), says the microbes come from the cow's gut, that they work without oxygen, that methane makers (archaea) are slow and need warmth. "It rots" alone is not enough.
- **gas-test:** all three cups tested the same way; explains milky = CO₂, that air shows no change and breath does, and that the part that burns is methane.
- **slurry-test:** germination percentages calculated correctly for every cup, with the water control, and a sensible rule for farmers (dilute it).
- **recommendation:** every number has working and units; the advice follows from the class's data, not from guesses. The gas estimate should be close to 1 m³ a day for 30 kg of dung.
- **hygiene:** gloves and handwashing every time, no flames, no touching faces at the digesters. If a student needed reminders, talk with them before signing.
