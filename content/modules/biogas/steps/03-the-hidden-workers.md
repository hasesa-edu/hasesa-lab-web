## Do: check the digesters (15 minutes)

It's been two to four days. Read every bottle (gloves on) and fill in your table. Then put your **total gas** for each bottle on the class board.

Look across the whole class:

- Which bottles have made the most gas so far?
- Has the water-only control moved at all? By how much?
- Do any bottles smell sour or rotten? Has any bottle made a lot of gas fast and then stopped?

## Notice something puzzling

Nobody put any microbes in the bottles. Nobody took the oxygen out. Yet something is making gas, and the bottles in the cold are hardly working at all. Who is doing it, and why do they need it warm?

## Theory: microbes and life without oxygen (20 minutes)

Read **Microbes** and look again at **Fermentation and respiration**. Then read this.

A cow's stomach (the **rumen**) is a big, warm tank with no oxygen. Billions of microbes live there and help the cow digest grass. They come out in the dung, still alive. When you seal the dung in a bottle, they carry on working. The little oxygen in the bottle is used up within a day by the microbes that need it. After that, only the ones that live **without oxygen** (anaerobic microbes) can work.

They work as three teams, in a chain:

1. **The breakers.** Bacteria release enzymes that cut big molecules (cellulose and starch from grass, proteins, fats) into small ones like sugars.
2. **The acid makers.** Other bacteria ferment the sugars into acids (mostly acetic acid, the acid in vinegar), carbon dioxide and hydrogen. This is fast.
3. **The methane makers.** Tiny single-celled microbes called **archaea** turn the acid, and the hydrogen with carbon dioxide, into **methane**, $\text{CH}_4$:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_4 + \text{CO}_2$$

$$\text{CO}_2 + 4\,\text{H}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_4 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The methane makers are slow. They divide only every few days, they hate oxygen, they like it warm (about 35 °C, like inside a cow), and they stop if the slurry gets too acidic. So a digester often starts with a burst of carbon dioxide from the acid makers, and only later makes much methane. That burst is why food scraps alone can bubble fast, turn sour and then stop: the acid makers win, the acid builds up, and the methane makers give up.

**Biogas** is the mixture that comes out: usually about 50–70% methane, 30–50% carbon dioxide, and a trace of hydrogen sulfide, which smells of rotten eggs.

## Do it better: the maths of a digester (25 minutes)

Put the three teams together for a simple sugar, glucose, and you get the overall change:

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 3\,\text{CH}_4 + 3\,\text{CO}_2$$

1. **Check the balance.** Count the C, H and O atoms on each side. Are they equal?
2. **How much gas?** One mole of any gas takes up about 24 L at room temperature. One mole of glucose is 180 g. How many litres of gas (methane plus carbon dioxide) does 180 g of glucose make? How many litres per gram?
3. **What fraction is methane** according to this equation? Real biogas is often richer in methane than this. Suggest one reason. (Hint: carbon dioxide dissolves in water. Methane hardly does.)
4. **How much food is in your bottle?** Fresh dung is about 80% water. Of the 20% that is solid, about 80% is organic matter the microbes can eat, at least slowly. How many grams of organic matter are in 700 g of dung?
5. **How much has been eaten?** Suppose your standard bottle makes 2 L of gas in two weeks. Using your answer to question 2, how many grams of food is that? What fraction of the organic matter is it?

Your answer to question 5 should surprise you. What does it tell you about how long a real digester has to keep the dung inside?
