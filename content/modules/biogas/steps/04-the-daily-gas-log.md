## Every day: read your digesters (5 minutes a day, about 50 minutes in all)

Your team takes turns. At about the **same time** each day, one person (gloves on) does this:

1. Read the thermometer next to the bottles.
2. **Water collector:** read the water level against your lines. **Balloon:** wrap a string around the widest part, mark it, and measure the length on a ruler. That is the circumference, $C$.
3. Write the reading, the time and anything unusual: a leak, a crust on the slurry, a smell, a balloon that went down.
4. If a water collector is nearly full of gas, write down the reading first. Then lift the tube out, refill the collector with water exactly as on day 0, put the tube back, and carry on counting from your last total.
5. Wash your hands.

If a day is missed (a weekend, a holiday), it's fine. Write the day number honestly; don't make up a reading.

## Balloon maths (if you used balloons)

A blown-up balloon is close to a sphere. Its radius is $r = \dfrac{C}{2\pi}$ and its volume is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$. Put them together:

$$V = \frac{C^3}{6\pi^2}$$

**Try it.** A balloon has a circumference of 40 cm. Show that its volume is about 1,080 cm³ (1.08 L). Then work out the volume for your own balloon every day.

## Notice something puzzling

Look at the water-only control. Its reading goes up a little on sunny afternoons and back down by the morning, though it makes no gas at all. Why? What does that tell you about readings of only 10–30 mL?

## Graph (40 minutes, after at least 7 days)

Read **Graphs and relationships** first.

1. Fill in the **gas today** and **total gas** columns for every bottle. Gas today = today's total minus yesterday's.
2. On one sheet of graph paper, plot **total gas (mL)** on the y-axis against **day** on the x-axis, for all three of your bottles. Use a different symbol for each, and a key.
3. Draw a smooth curve through each set of points. Don't join the dots with a ruler.
4. Most curves have three parts: a flat start (the **lag**), a steep middle, and sometimes a flattening at the end. Label them on your graph.
5. Find the **fastest rate** for each bottle: draw a straight line along the steepest part and work out its gradient:

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{change in total gas}}{\text{change in days}} \quad (\text{mL per day})$$

6. Copy the class's **standard bottle** results from the board. Work out their mean total on the same day, and their range. Is the difference between your three bottles bigger than the range of the standard bottles? If not, you can't be sure it's real.

## Theory: what changes the rate?

Re-read **Rates of reaction**. For each factor below, say which bottle in the class tests it, and what you'd predict:

- **Temperature:** warmer microbes work faster, up to about 35–40 °C.
- **Surface area:** chopped and mashed scraps expose more surface to enzymes.
- **Concentration:** thicker slurry has more food per bottle, but is it too thick for gas to escape?

## Record

Your full gas log, your graph with the three parts labelled, the fastest rate of each bottle with working, and three sentences comparing your bottles. Keep reading every day until your guide says stop.
