# Market Detective: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. **Students must never see this file**, especially the answer key. You do not need to be a chemistry specialist: every test here is safe, cheap and works with kitchen materials, and every result you should expect is written down below.

The module is about 10 hours, best run as five or six sessions of 1.5–2 hours. It assumes up to 16 students in four teams of four. Scale quantities up or down as needed.

---

## 1. The story in one paragraph

Students receive a "case file" of 16 samples: honey, milk, berbere and kibe, each labelled A–D. Some are genuine; some have been adulterated by you, following the recipes below. Students observe, form suspicions, then run five kinds of test (water-glass and density, iodine for starch, paper chromatography for dyes, red-cabbage indicator for pH, sedimentation for grit). Each test brings in a concept unit at the moment they need it. They finish with an evidence table, a written case report and a short presentation.

## 3. Preparing the case file

Make every sample in a clean jar, label it with the **student label only** (e.g. "Honey B"), and write the secret recipe in your own notebook. Do this away from students.

**Before anything else, test your "genuine" foods:**

- Run the chromatography test (step 4) on your genuine berbere. If its water extract gives a bright red spot that travels up the paper, it may already be dyed. Buy from a different source.
- Run the iodine test on your genuine milk and kibe. They should stay yellow-brown.
- Measure the density of your genuine honey and milk using the student method. Record your numbers: these are your reference values.

### Honey (make the day before or on the day; keeps for weeks)

First make a **thin sugar syrup**: 100 g sugar + 100 g water, warmed and stirred until completely clear, then cooled to room temperature. (Density about 1.23 g/mL.)

| Label | What it is | Recipe (about 200 mL each) |
|---|---|---|
| **Honey A** | FAKE: half syrup | 100 mL genuine honey + 100 mL thin syrup, stirred very thoroughly (5 minutes) |
| **Honey B** | Genuine | 200 mL genuine honey |
| **Honey C** | Genuine | 200 mL genuine honey (same honey as B) |
| **Honey D** | FAKE: mostly syrup | 160 mL thin syrup + 40 mL genuine honey + 1–2 teaspoons of very strong black tea (to darken it to a honey colour). Stir thoroughly. |

### Milk (make fresh on the day of every milk session; keep cold)

Milk spoils in a day at room temperature. Make a new batch, from the same recipes, for each session that uses milk (steps 2, 3 and 5). Keep it in a fridge or cool box, then let it reach room temperature for 30 minutes before density measurements.

| Label | What it is | Recipe (about 600 mL each) |
|---|---|---|
| **Milk A** | Genuine | 600 mL whole milk |
| **Milk B** | FAKE: watered | 450 mL milk + 150 mL water (25% water) |
| **Milk C** | FAKE: watered, then thickened with flour | 400 mL milk + 200 mL water + 2 level tablespoons (about 16 g) wheat flour or cornstarch. Whisk the flour into a little of the water first to make a smooth paste, then add. Shake well before handing out. |
| **Milk D** | FAKE: baking soda added (to hide souring) | 600 mL milk + 1 level teaspoon (about 5 g) baking soda, stirred until dissolved |

Please do a trial of Milk D with the cabbage indicator before the session (see section 5). If D is not clearly bluer than A, increase to 1½ teaspoons.

### Berbere (make up to 2 weeks ahead; keeps dry)

**Making brick dust (you, not students):** outdoors, wearing a dust mask and safety glasses, put a piece of red brick inside a cloth bag and crush it with a hammer, or rub two brick pieces together over paper. Sieve through a fine tea strainer. You need about 20 g of fine, red-brown powder.

| Label | What it is | Recipe (about 100 g each) |
|---|---|---|
| **Berbere A** | Genuine | 100 g genuine berbere |
| **Berbere B** | FAKE: brick dust | 80 g berbere + 20 g sieved brick dust. Shake in a closed jar for 2 minutes. |
| **Berbere C** | FAKE: synthetic red dye | 100 g berbere spread on a plate; sprinkle 1 teaspoon liquid red food colouring (or ¼ teaspoon gel dissolved in 1 teaspoon water) over it while mixing with a fork. Leave to dry for 12–24 hours, then crumble and shake in a jar. |
| **Berbere D** | FAKE: flour filler + dye to hide it | 75 g berbere + 25 g wheat flour, mixed; then add 1½ teaspoons red food colouring as for C. Dry 12–24 hours, crumble. |

### Kibe (make up to 3 days ahead; keep in the fridge)

Soften the kibe at room temperature first so it mixes smoothly.

| Label | What it is | Recipe (about 100 g each) |
|---|---|---|
| **Kibe A** | FAKE: mashed potato | Boil and peel a small potato, mash it very smoothly with a fork (no salt, no butter), and let it cool. Mix 20 g of mashed potato into 80 g kibe until smooth. |
| **Kibe B** | Genuine | 100 g kibe |
| **Kibe C** | FAKE: flour | 90 g kibe + 10 g wheat flour, worked in thoroughly with a fork |
| **Kibe D** | Genuine | 100 g kibe |

Before each session, take kibe out of the fridge 30 minutes early.

## 5. Expected results and how to make each test work

### Water-glass test (step 2)
Genuine honey drops as a lump and sits on the bottom; after a gentle swirl it stays mostly together. Syrupy honey (A, D) starts clouding and streaking within the minute and spreads on swirling. This test is a good starter but is **not reliable on its own** (thick genuine honey and very thick syrup can behave alike), which is why we move to density.

### Density
- Honey: 20.0 mL in a syringe; pull slowly, push out air bubbles; wait for the last drop. Students may get 1–3% low because honey sticks inside the syringe. This does not hide the fakes (A and D are 7–11% lower).
- Milk: the **bottle method** is much more accurate than a syringe. Fill exactly to the brim each time, cap, wipe dry. Milk can be poured back into its jar after weighing, to save milk (it is not for drinking anyway). Results to expect for a 250 mL bottle: A ≈ 257.5 g of milk, B ≈ 255.6 g, C ≈ 257 g, D ≈ 258.8 g. The A–B difference is only about 2 g, so the scale must read at least to 0.5 g, ideally 0.1 g.
- Temperature: all samples should be at room temperature.

### Iodine (step 3)
- **Dilute first.** Tincture of iodine (2%): 1 part iodine + 4 parts water. Povidone-iodine (Betadine 10%): 1 part + 9 parts water. Make 30 mL and put it in labelled dropper bottles.
- **Too strong?** If the plain-water negative control looks dark brown, dilute more. Blue-black versus dark brown is hard to tell when iodine is too strong.
- **Too weak or old?** If cornstarch does not go blue-black, use more drops or a fresh bottle. This is exactly why the positive control comes first.
- Milk C: blue-black within seconds; the colour may concentrate in the flour that settles. Shake Milk C before pouring.
- Kibe: the warm water softens the fat. Iodine dissolves in fat and goes orange: that is "no starch". Look for blue-black specks and patches, especially on pale lumps.
- Berbere: genuine berbere often shows a few dark specks because ginger and other spices contain some natural starch. Berbere D should look clearly and overall darker. Treat this test as supporting evidence only, and use Berbere A as the control.

### Chromatography (step 4)
- **Spots too faint:** concentrate the extract (less water, more powder, let it settle, take liquid from the top), and spot 5–6 times on the same dot, drying in between.
- **Spots washed away:** the start line was below the solvent. Keep about 1 cm of solvent and draw the start line at 2 cm.
- **Spots spread into big blobs:** spots too wet. Use a toothpick and tiny dots.
- Coffee filter paper and a pinch of salt in water work well. Genuine berbere: a faint yellow-orange smudge that barely moves. C and D: a red spot at the same height as K (Rf typically 0.4–0.8, depending on your paper and dye; what matters is the match on the same strip). Many red food colours are themselves a blend, so K may split into two spots; C and D should show the same pattern.

### Red cabbage (step 5)
- **Make it strong.** Use plenty of cabbage and just enough hot water to cover. A deep purple juice works better than a pale one.
- **Colours ambiguous in milk:** milk makes everything pastel. Always compare side by side on white paper with Milk A as the control, with equal volumes of milk and juice. Genuine milk: lilac/mauve-pink. Milk D: noticeably bluer, blue-grey or lavender-blue. Photograph them together.
- **Optional known positive:** in a separate cup, add a pinch of baking soda to some genuine milk; it should match D. This is a good fair-test discussion, but it gives the answer away, so use it only after teams have recorded their own results.
- Fizz: one teaspoon of vinegar into 10 mL milk. All milks curdle a little. D foams with tiny bubbles (carbon dioxide). If it is hard to see, use a narrow glass.
- The juice keeps 1–2 days in a fridge; it goes off and smells after that.

### Sedimentation (step 6)
Brick dust sinks to the bottom within a few seconds as a red-brown, sandy layer. Genuine berbere mostly floats at first, then slowly clouds the water; after decanting three times there is only a thin film of sediment. Berbere B leaves a clear, gritty layer several millimetres thick. Drying: overnight on filter paper in a warm, dry place. If you cannot dry it in time, compare layer heights against a ruler in identical glasses.

Expected percentages: B about 15–25% above the control (losses during decanting make it come out a little low). Accept anything clearly above the control.

## 6. Session plan

### Step 1: Open the case file (45 min)
- **5 min.** Present the "case file" box with drama: a sealed envelope, the note, the jars. "Someone has been cheating shoppers in our market. We need proof."
- **5 min. Safety briefing** (section 8). Gloves and glasses on before opening anything.
- **25 min.** Teams observe all 16 samples side by side on white paper. Circulate and push for observations, not conclusions: "What do you actually see? Write that down."
- **10 min.** Teams write a suspect list and a test plan. Ask: "Which of these properties could a faker easily copy? Which would be hard to copy?" Collect or photograph the suspect lists; you will bring them back in step 7.
- Homework: Properties of matter.

### Step 2: Heavy honey, thin milk (90 min)
- **15 min.** Water-glass test. Ask: "Can we turn 'spreads faster' into a number?"
- **15 min.** Teach or discuss density with the formula and the table of densities. Do the worked example on the board. Show how to rearrange $\rho = m/V$ to $V = m/\rho$ and $m = \rho V$.
- **30 min.** Honey density with syringes. Check every team's calculation and units.
- **30 min.** Milk density with the bottle method (run as two stations if you have two scales and bottles). Ask: "Why use water to find the volume?"
- End by pointing at Milk C: "It looks thin, but its density is almost normal. How?"

### Step 3: The starch spy (90 min)
- **20 min.** Cornstarch and water controls, then the four milks. Ask: "Why did we test plain water at all?"
- **15 min.** Chemical tests unit: controls, fair tests, contamination, dilution.
- **30 min.** Kibe test (you add the warm water) and the melt test.
- **20 min.** Berbere challenge. Expect confusion here: it is the point. Ask: "Genuine berbere has a few specks too. So what does a positive result mean? What do we compare to?"
- **5 min.** Record.

### Step 4: The colour crime (90 min)
- **20 min.** Water extracts. Ask: "When you cook wot, does berbere colour the oil or the water?"
- **15 min.** Chromatography unit, including how to measure Rf from the start line.
- **45 min.** Run the chromatogram. Check start lines are in pencil and above the solvent before the paper goes in.
- **10 min.** Measure Rf and record. Mention Sudan dyes and why food-safety labs care.

### Step 5: Acid or base? (90 min)
- **25 min.** You make the cabbage juice (you handle the kettle and hot water). Teams make the colour key with lemon/vinegar, water and baking soda.
- **20 min.** Acids, bases and pH unit. Emphasise the power-of-ten idea.
- **35 min.** Milk tests, fizz check, pH paper if available.
- **10 min.** Ask: "Milk D passed the density test and the starch test. What does that tell us about using only one test?"

### Step 6: Grit at the bottom (75 min)
- **15 min.** Sink test.
- **15 min.** Mixtures and separation unit, with the worked percentage example.
- **40 min.** Decanting, filtration, set sediment to dry. If you need the dried mass for the verdict, start this early in the session, or weigh at the start of step 7.
- **5 min.** Teams list which samples are still uncertain.

### Step 7: The verdict (120 min)
- **40 min.** Evidence board; allow re-tests of any uncertain sample.
- **40 min.** Write the case report.
- **35 min.** Presentations (about 5 minutes per team plus questions), then open the sealed answer key together.
- **5 min.** Clean-up and sign-off.

## 7. Conceptual sticking points and questions to ask

- **"Heavier means denser."** A big jar is heavier than a spoon of the same honey but has the same density. Ask: "If I pour half the honey out, what happens to its density?"
- **mL versus g.** Students mix up volume and mass. Insist on units at every line of working.
- **Volumes of mixtures.** In our problems we assume volumes add; in reality they add *almost* exactly for these mixtures. This is fine at this level.
- **Observation versus conclusion.** Push "What did you see?" before "What does it mean?"
- **Controls feel like a waste of time.** Show what happens without one: a team that sees a few dark specks in genuine berbere and calls it "fake". Ask: "How do you know genuine berbere doesn't do that too?"
- **One test is enough.** Milk C (passes density) and Milk D (passes density and starch) are deliberately designed to break this belief.
- **pH goes "the wrong way".** Lower pH means *more* acidic. Ask: "Which has more H⁺, pH 3 or pH 6? How many times more?"
- **Rf measured from the bottom of the paper.** It must be from the start line, to the centre of the spot.
- **Dissolved means gone.** Use the sugar-in-water mass example: hidden is not gone.

Good questions to ask any team: "What was your control?" "Could contamination explain that?" "If you ran it again, would you get the same answer?" "Which piece of evidence would convince a judge?"

## 8. Safety briefing (read aloud at the start of every session)

1. **Never taste or eat any sample.** Some fakes contain brick dust and other things that are not food. Spoons and cups are shared and can carry contamination. Nothing goes in mouths, nothing goes home.
2. **Gloves and safety glasses on** whenever samples or iodine are out.
3. **Iodine** stains skin and clothes and is harmful if swallowed. We only use it diluted, from dropper bottles. Keep it away from eyes. If it splashes on skin, wash with water; if in eyes, rinse with lots of clean water for 10 minutes and tell the guide.
4. **Hot water** from the kettle is handled only by the guide. Hot cups stay on the table.
5. **Berbere** burns eyes and nose. Don't rub your eyes; waft, don't sniff; wash hands with soap after.
6. **Label everything** as soon as it is filled.
7. **Spills:** tell the guide, wipe up straight away (milk and honey make floors slippery).
8. **Clean-up:** liquids down the sink with plenty of running water; solids (berbere, brick dust, kibe, curdled milk) into a bin bag, not the sink, because they block drains. Wash all cups and spoons with soap. Wash hands before leaving.

Your own extra precautions: make brick dust outdoors with a dust mask; keep milk and kibe samples cold and make milk fresh each session; check no student has a known iodine allergy (rare, but ask); keep the iodine stock bottle yourself.

## 9. How to judge each sign-off item

- **verdicts.** Compare the team's final table with the answer key. All 16 must be correct (genuine / faked, and the type of faking as described in "Accept as fully correct"). Each verdict must cite at least one specific result (a number, a colour versus control, an Rf, a sediment observation). If one or two are wrong but the reasoning is honest and evidence-based, let the team re-test that sample before signing off.
- **fair-test.** Ask the student: "Show me one test where you used a control. What was it and why?" A good answer names the control (cornstarch as a known positive, Milk A or Berbere A as a known genuine, plain water as a negative) and explains that it shows what "yes" or "no" looks like, or proves the test works. Also check that amounts were kept equal.
- **density.** Ask to see the working: mass, volume and $\rho = m/V$ with units, for at least one honey or milk sample. The value should be within about 2% of your own reference measurement for that sample (for genuine honey, roughly 1.36–1.45 g/mL; for genuine milk, 1.028–1.034 g/mL).
- **chromatography.** The student shows the chromatogram, measures one Rf from the start line to the spot's centre and to the solvent front, and calculates it correctly (check it yourself; ±0.05 is fine). They must explain that chilli's natural red is oil-soluble and barely moves in water, while synthetic dyes are water-soluble and run, and that matching K on the same paper is the evidence.
- **presented.** The team presents clearly, explains what each faker did and how they proved it, answers at least one question about controls or contamination, and names a result that surprised them. Every team member should speak at least once.

## 10. Notes for experts reviewing this draft

- The honey water-glass test is popular but scientifically weak; it is kept as a hook and backed up by density.
- Baking soda quantity for Milk D (1 teaspoon per 600 mL) should be trialled; milk is buffered, so the colour shift may need more.
- Genuine berbere's response to iodine varies with recipe (ginger content); the berbere starch test is presented as supporting evidence only.
- Food colouring stands in for illegal Sudan dyes; the chromatography behaviour (water-soluble, runs) is similar for most permitted red dyes.
