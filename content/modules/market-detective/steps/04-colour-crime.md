## Do: shake out the colour (20 minutes)

Put half a teaspoon of each berbere (A–D) into a labelled cup. Add 2 teaspoons of water. Stir well for a minute, then let it settle for five minutes.

Now look at the **water** above the powder, not the powder.

- Which cups have pale, yellow-orange water?
- Which have water that is suspiciously bright red?

Next, dip the tip of a strip of white paper into each cup for a second. Compare the stains.

## Notice something puzzling

All four berberes look red as powders. But real chilli's red colour **barely dissolves in water**. It is an oily colour: think of how berbere turns the oil red when you cook wot, not the water. So where is the bright red in the water coming from?

A suspicion is not proof. Is that red the same as a shop food dye? Is it one colour or several mixed?

## Theory: chromatography

Read **Chromatography**. You will learn:

- why different dyes travel different distances up the paper,
- why the start line is drawn in **pencil** and must stay **above** the solvent,
- how to calculate and compare **Rf values**: $R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by spot}}{\text{distance moved by solvent}}$.

## Do it better: run a chromatogram (45 minutes)

1. Cut a strip of coffee filter or blotting paper about 4 cm wide and 12 cm tall (or one wide sheet for all samples).
2. With a **pencil**, draw a start line 2 cm from the bottom. Mark five dots along it, 1.5 cm apart, and label them in pencil below the line: A, B, C, D, and **K** for the known food colour.
3. Make concentrated extracts: let the berbere water from earlier settle, then use a dropper to take some of the clear liquid from the top. For dot K, use one drop of red food colouring mixed with five drops of water.
4. With a toothpick or the thin tip of a dropper, put a small dot of each extract on its mark. Let it dry (blow gently or wave it). Repeat **four or five times** on the same dot, drying between each. Small and strong is the aim.
5. Put about 1 cm of salty water (a pinch of salt in a cup of water) into a clear glass. Stand the paper in it so the start line is **above** the water. Clip the top to a pencil laid across the glass so it hangs straight.
6. Wait until the water is about 2 cm from the top (10–20 minutes). Take it out and **immediately** mark the solvent front with pencil.
7. When dry, measure from the start line to the solvent front and to the centre of each spot. Calculate every Rf.

## Record

Draw or photograph your chromatogram. In the evidence table, add a **dye** column: Rf values, and whether each sample matches **K**.

Ask yourself: does a sample that leaves bright spots matching K count as proof it was dyed? What else would you want to check?

## Why it matters

Some fakers use illegal industrial dyes (like the "Sudan" dyes) that can damage health. Our food colour is a safe stand-in, but the method is the same one food-safety labs start with.
