## Do: the water-glass test (15 minutes)

Fill four clear glasses with room-temperature water, labelled Honey A–D. Using a clean spoon for each, gently drop one teaspoon of honey into the middle of each glass. **Do not stir.** Watch for one minute.

- Does the honey sink as a lump and sit at the bottom?
- Does it start to cloud and spread out into the water?
- After one minute, swirl each glass gently once. What happens?

Photograph each glass. Record what you see.

## Notice something puzzling

Real honey is mostly sugar and only about 17% water. It is thick and **dense**, so it drops as a lump and dissolves slowly. Honey stretched with thin sugar syrup is runnier and less dense, so it spreads out faster.

But "spreads faster" is a description, not a number. Two people might disagree. A detective wants a measurement.

## Theory: density

Read the **Density** unit now (or with your guide). The key idea:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Real honey is about **1.36–1.45 g/mL**. Thin sugar syrup is about 1.23 g/mL. Whole milk is **1.028–1.034 g/mL**. Water is 1.00 g/mL. Mixing in a fake pulls the density towards the fake's density.

## Do it better: measure honey density (30 minutes)

1. Put a small cup on the scale and press **tare** (zero).
2. Slowly draw honey into a 20 mL syringe (no needle). Honey is thick, so be patient and push out any air bubbles. Fill to exactly 20.0 mL.
3. Squeeze all 20.0 mL into the tared cup. Wait for the last drop. Read the mass.
4. Calculate $\rho = m/V$. Show your working with units.
5. Repeat for each honey sample with a **clean** syringe, or wash and dry it carefully between samples.

Check: if the mass is 28.4 g, then $\rho = 28.4 \div 20.0 = 1.42$ g/mL. Genuine range!

## Do it better again: milk density with the bottle method (30 minutes)

Milk is only a little denser than water, so we need more care.

1. Weigh the empty, dry bottle with its cap: $m_{\text{empty}}$.
2. Fill it to the very brim with water, screw on the cap, wipe the outside dry, weigh it: $m_{\text{water}}$. The volume is $V = m_{\text{water}} - m_{\text{empty}}$ in mL (because water is 1.00 g/mL).
3. Empty and shake it as dry as you can. Fill to the brim with Milk A, cap, wipe, weigh.
4. Mass of milk = full mass − empty mass. Calculate $\rho$.
5. Rinse with water and repeat for Milk B, C and D.

Keep all samples at room temperature so temperature does not change the result.

**Challenge.** If genuine milk is 1.030 g/mL and a sample is 1.020 g/mL, what fraction of it is added water? (Hint: the Density unit has a worked method.)

## Record

Add a **density** column to your evidence table. Mark any sample outside the genuine range.

## The question it leaves

One milk sample may look a bit watery but still give a nearly normal density. How could a faker add water *and* keep the density up? Keep that sample on your suspect list. The next test may explain it.
