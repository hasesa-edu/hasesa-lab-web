## Do: a first iodine test (20 minutes)

Your guide will give you **dilute iodine** in a dropper bottle. Gloves and safety glasses on. Iodine stains skin and clothes.

Start with two cups:

- Cup 1: a pinch of cornstarch stirred into a spoon of water.
- Cup 2: a spoon of plain water.

Add 2 drops of dilute iodine to each. Cup 1 turns **blue-black**. Cup 2 stays **yellow-brown**.

Now test the four **milk** samples. Put 10 mL of each into a labelled cup (use a syringe so the amount is the same). Add exactly 2 drops of iodine to each. Swirl. Wait one minute. Photograph them side by side.

## Notice something puzzling

Milk has no starch naturally, so any blue-black is suspicious. Look back at your density results: is the milk that turns blue-black the one whose density seemed nearly normal even though it looked thin? Why would a faker add flour to watered milk?

And another puzzle: why did we bother testing cornstarch and plain water first, when the case is about milk?

## Theory: chemical tests

Read **Chemical tests**. You will learn:

- why iodine turns blue-black only with starch (it slides inside the coiled starch chains),
- what a **positive control** (cornstarch) and a **negative control** (water, or known genuine milk) are, and why a result without them is weak evidence,
- what makes a test **fair**, and how a dirty spoon can create a **false positive**,
- how to dilute iodine using $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$.

## Do it better: test the kibe (30 minutes)

Butter is fat, and iodine dissolves in fat, which turns it orange. We need a fair method.

1. Put a pea-sized lump of each kibe sample (A–D) into a labelled cup, using a **separate clean spoon** for each.
2. Ask your guide to add about 10 mL of warm (not boiling) water to each cup. Stir until the butter softens and spreads.
3. Prepare your controls: one cup with genuine kibe if you have one you trust, and one cup with a pinch of cornstarch.
4. Add exactly 3 drops of iodine to every cup. Stir. Wait one minute.
5. Look for blue-black specks or patches, especially in any pale lumps that sink.

**Melt test (extra evidence).** With the guide, warm a teaspoon of each kibe in a small metal cup over warm water. Genuine kibe melts into a clear golden oil. Does any sample leave a cloudy white layer or lumps at the bottom?

## Do it better: the berbere challenge (20 minutes)

Test a quarter teaspoon of each berbere in 10 mL of water with 3 drops of iodine. This one is harder: berbere is dark red, and some real spices like ginger contain a little natural starch. So genuine berbere may show **a few** dark specks.

This is exactly why a control matters. Compare every sample to the genuine-looking ones. Is one sample **much** darker and more blue-black overall?

## Record

Add a **starch** column to the evidence table: none / a little / strong, with photos. Write one sentence per food explaining which control you used and why.
