## Do: the sink test (15 minutes)

Fill four tall clear glasses with water, labelled Berbere A–D. Sprinkle **one level teaspoon** of each berbere gently on the surface of its glass. Do not stir yet. Watch for 30 seconds.

Then stir each glass for 10 seconds with its own clean spoon and stop. Time how long it takes for particles to reach the bottom. Watch from the side at eye level.

- Which powders mostly float or stay cloudy?
- Does any sample drop a layer of reddish-brown grit to the bottom within seconds?

## Notice something puzzling

All four powders are red and fine. Why does one part of a sample race to the bottom while the rest hangs in the water? Think back to density: what do you know about things that sink fast?

## Theory: mixtures and separation

Read **Mixtures and separation**. You will learn:

- the difference between a **solution**, a **suspension** and a **colloid** (and which one milk is),
- how **sedimentation**, **decanting**, **filtration** and **evaporation** pull mixtures apart,
- why brick dust (about 2.6 g/cm³, and it does not dissolve) sinks so much faster than spice particles that hold oil and air,
- how to estimate the **percentage** of an adulterant using a control.

## Do it better: measure the grit (45 minutes)

1. Weigh 5.0 g of each berbere into a labelled glass. Add about 150 mL of water. Stir for 30 seconds, then wait exactly **2 minutes**.
2. **Decant**: pour off the cloudy water and floating spice gently into a waste bowl, leaving the sediment behind.
3. Add fresh water, swirl, wait 1 minute, decant again. Repeat two more times. Fast-sinking grit stays; light spice washes away.
4. Rub a little of the sediment between gloved fingers. Gritty like sand?
5. Tip the sediment onto a weighed piece of filter paper (filtration). Leave it to dry until the next session, or on a sunny windowsill.
6. Weigh the dry sediment on its paper and subtract the mass of the paper.
7. Use the genuine-looking sample as your **control**:

$$\text{added grit (\%)} \approx \frac{m_{\text{sediment}} - m_{\text{control sediment}}}{m_{\text{sample}}} \times 100$$

If drying is not possible in time, compare the **height** of the sediment layers in identical glasses instead, and photograph them against a ruler.

## Record

Add a **sediment** column to the evidence table: sediment height or mass, texture, and your percentage estimate.

## Look back

You now have five kinds of evidence. For each sample, how many separate tests point the same way? Which samples are still uncertain, and what could you re-test before the verdict?
