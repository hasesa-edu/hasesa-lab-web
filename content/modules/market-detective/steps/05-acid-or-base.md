## Do: make a colour-changing potion (25 minutes)

With your guide: chop about two big handfuls of red cabbage into small pieces and put them in a heat-proof jug. **The guide** pours hot water from the kettle over it, just enough to cover. Stir and wait 10–15 minutes until the water is deep purple. Strain it through a tea strainer into a clean jar and let it cool.

Now test three **known** liquids in labelled clear cups. Use 20 mL of each and add 2 mL (one dropper-full) of cabbage juice:

- **Lemon juice or vinegar** (known acid)
- **Plain water** (neutral)
- **A pinch of baking soda in water** (known base)

Line them up. What colours do you see? Photograph them: this is your **colour key**.

## Notice something puzzling

The same purple juice turned three different colours. Nothing was added except lemon, water or baking soda. What do lemon and baking soda have that water does not?

And why would a milk detective care?

## Theory: acids, bases and pH

Read **Acids, bases and pH**. You will learn:

- what acids and bases are, and what happens when they **neutralise** each other,
- the **pH scale**, and why pH 5 is 100 times more acidic than pH 7,
- how red cabbage works as an **indicator**,
- why fresh milk is slightly acidic (pH 6.6–6.8), why souring milk gets more acidic, and why a dishonest seller might add **baking soda** to hide it.

## Do it better: test the milk (35 minutes)

1. Put 20 mL of each milk sample A–D into labelled clear cups (use a syringe so the amounts are equal).
2. Add exactly 2 mL of cabbage juice to each. Swirl gently.
3. Place them in a row on white paper, next to your colour key. Milk makes every colour paler, so compare the milks **with each other** as well as with the key.
4. Which milk is noticeably **bluer or greener** than the others?

**Fizz check.** Add one teaspoon of vinegar to 10 mL of each milk in fresh cups and swirl. The milk will curdle a little (that is normal). Look closely: does any sample foam or give tiny bubbles? What gas would that be, and what does it tell you was in the milk?

**Extra.** If your guide has pH paper, dip a strip into each milk and read its pH.

## Record

Add a **pH / indicator** column to the evidence table: the colour, your estimated pH, and the fizz result.

## The question it leaves

The baking-soda milk might look and weigh like perfectly normal milk. What does that tell you about relying on one test?
