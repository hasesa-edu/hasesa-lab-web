## The story (10 minutes)

About 240 BCE, Eratosthenes ran the great library at Alexandria in Egypt. He heard that at noon on midsummer's day in Syene (today's Aswan), far to the south up the Nile, the sun shone straight down a deep well and upright pillars cast no shadow. On the same day in Alexandria, a pillar **did** cast a shadow. The sun's rays came in about 7° from overhead.

The sun is so far away that its rays arrive **parallel** everywhere on Earth. So if the angle is different in two places, the ground itself must be **curved**. And 7° is about $\frac{1}{50}$ of a full circle, so the distance from Syene to Alexandria must be about $\frac{1}{50}$ of the way round the Earth.

## Theory: the geometry (15 minutes)

Draw a big circle (Earth) and two dots on it, A and B, one due north of the other. Draw parallel sun rays arriving at both. Draw a line from each dot to the centre of the Earth.

- The angle between the sun's ray and the vertical at A is $z_A$; at B it is $z_B$.
- The angle between the two lines at Earth's centre is $z_B - z_A$. (Alternate angles between parallel lines.)

So

$$\frac{\text{distance from A to B}}{\text{circumference}} = \frac{z_B - z_A}{360°}, \qquad \text{circumference} = \frac{360° \times \text{distance}}{z_B - z_A}.$$

That second form comes from rearranging the first. Check it with **Rearranging formulas**.

## Do: the Ethiopian experiment (35 minutes)

Use **Option A** if your class has a partner in another city. Use **Option B** otherwise, or as well.

**Option A: a partner city.** Before this session, your guide arranges for a partner (another school, or a relative) at least 300 km north or south of Addis, such as in Mekelle, Bahir Dar, Gondar, Hawassa or Arba Minch. On the **same day**, both places measure the noon shadow of a vertical 1.00 m stick, exactly as in step 5, and share the result by phone.

Find the **north–south** distance between the two cities on a map: measure how far one is north of the other with a ruler (not along the road, and ignoring how far east or west), and convert with the scale bar.

**Option B: given data.** On 21 December, students in three cities measured the noon shadow of a vertical 1.000 m stick. All three shadows pointed north.

| City | Noon shadow of a 1.000 m stick | North–south distance from Hawassa |
|---|---|---|
| Hawassa | 0.589 m | 0 km |
| Addis Ababa | 0.636 m | 219 km |
| Mekelle | 0.752 m | 716 km |

For each pair of cities:

1. Work out each city's $z = \tan^{-1}(s/h)$, to 2 decimal places.
2. Find the difference in $z$.
3. Calculate the circumference.

## Notice something puzzling (10 minutes)

The modern value of Earth's circumference, measured round the poles, is about **40,000 km**.

1. Which pair of cities gave the best answer? Why do cities that are close together give a less reliable result? (Try it: change Hawassa's shadow by just 2 mm, to 0.591 m, and redo the Hawassa–Addis calculation.)
2. Your guide's data was given to the nearest millimetre. Your own shadows in step 5 were probably only good to about half a centimetre. What would that do to your answer?

## Record (20 minutes)

Write up your estimate of the Earth's circumference as a short report:

1. **Method:** a labelled diagram of Earth, the two cities and the parallel sun rays.
2. **Results:** your table, with working for one pair in full.
3. **Answer:** your best estimate, and how far it is from 40,000 km as a percentage.
4. **Errors:** at least two things that could make your answer wrong, and which way each would push it.
5. **One more fact:** from your circumference, how far is 1° of latitude, in km?
