## Plan the day (10 minutes)

Today your team marks the tip of the shadow **every 30 minutes** from about 9:30 am to 3:00 pm (EAT), and **every 10 minutes** from 11:50 am to 12:50 pm, when the shadow changes fastest in direction. Your guide will give you a rota so someone from the team is always free to take a reading.

Lay your paper sheet around the foot of the stick and hold it down with stones, or work in chalk on concrete. Draw a small cross at the exact centre of the stick's foot.

Set up a table in your notebook:

| Time (EAT) | Ethiopian time | Shadow length (cm) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9:30 | 3:30 | | |

## Do: mark the shadow tip (45 minutes, spread across the day)

At each reading:

1. Stand to the side so your own shadow doesn't touch the stick's.
2. Find the **centre** of the fuzzy tip. If you taped a cap on top, mark the centre of its round shadow.
3. Make a small dot, and write the time next to it.
4. Measure the length from the foot of the stick to the dot, to the nearest 0.5 cm.

Don't move the stick or the paper all day.

## Do: the equal-shadow method for north (20 minutes)

This is the shadow-tip method surveyors and travellers have used for thousands of years.

1. Tie the string to the foot of the stick and use it like a compass to draw **a circle** through your first morning dot (around 9:30). Draw two or three more circles through other morning dots.
2. In the afternoon, find where the shadow tip touches **the same circle again**. Mark it.
3. On each circle you now have a morning dot and an afternoon dot. Join them with a straight line. This line runs **east–west**. The morning dot is the **west** end, because the sun rises in the east and throws shadows west.
4. Find the midpoint of each east–west line, and draw a line from the foot of the stick through it. This is the **north–south line**, also called the **meridian**.

If your lines from different circles don't agree, take the middle one.

Check with a compass if you have one. In Addis, a compass points within a few degrees of true north.

## Notice something puzzling

Look at the shape your dots make, and which way the shortest shadow points. In Addis, from about September to mid-April, the noon shadow points **north**. From mid-April to late August it points **south**! Which is it today? Look back at the concept unit: why does it switch?

## Graph: when is solar noon? (25 minutes)

1. Plot **shadow length** (y-axis, cm) against **time** (x-axis, EAT). Use a sharp pencil and a ruler for the axes, and plot each point as a small cross.
2. Draw a **smooth curve** of best fit, not dot-to-dot.
3. Find the time of the **shortest** shadow. That is **solar noon**: the sun is due north or due south and highest in the sky.
4. Look at the north–south line on your plot. The shadow should cross it at the same moment.

The curve should be nearly **symmetrical**: the shadow at an hour before solar noon should be the same length as an hour after. Check yours.

## Record (20 minutes)

1. What time did you find for solar noon (EAT, and Ethiopian time)?
2. Your guide will tell you the predicted solar noon for Addis today. How far were you out? What made it hard to find the exact minute?
3. Why is solar noon not at 12:00 on the clock? (Hint: Addis is at 38.7°E, and EAT is set for 45°E. Earth turns 1° every 4 minutes.)
4. Photograph or carefully copy the plot. Keep the north–south line marked on the ground: you'll use it to aim your sundial in step 4.
