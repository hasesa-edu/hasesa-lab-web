# Sun Clock: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 10 hours, for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. Everything except step 3 needs **sunshine**, and steps 2, 5 and 7 need the hour around **solar noon** (12:09–12:39 EAT depending on the date). Plan sessions around that, and keep step 3 and the theory readings as rainy-day fallbacks.

The module works best from **October to March** (bega, the dry season), when skies are clearest. In kiremt (June–September) afternoon cloud and rain make a full day of shadow readings difficult; if you must run it then, do step 2 as two half-days.

---

## Session plan

| Session | Steps | Time | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 Twelve o'clock at dawn | 60 min | Any sunny morning or midday |
| 2 | 2 Follow the shadow | 120 min of student time across one day, about 9:30–15:00 EAT | A Saturday or a school day with a reading rota |
| 3 | 3 A spinning, tilted Earth | 75 min | Indoors; any time, good for a cloudy day |
| 4 | 4 Build a sun clock | 105 min | Late morning to early afternoon, so dials can be tested in sun |
| 5 | 5 How high is the sun? | 90 min | Must include solar noon: start about 45 min before it |
| 6 | 6 Measure the Earth | 90 min | Indoors is fine (option B). Option A needs a sunny noon in both cities, done earlier |
| 7 | 7 Sun clock day | 60 min | Spanning solar noon is nice but not essential |

Sessions 5 and 7 can be combined into one longer noon session if the timetable is tight.

## Before the module

### 1. Find the plots

Walk the compound at 9:30, 12:30 and 15:00 a few days before. Each team needs a level 2 × 2 m patch that is in **sun the whole time**. Between October and March, shadows point north-west in the morning, north at noon and north-east in the afternoon, so put each stick near the **south** edge of its patch. Concrete is ideal (chalk marks, no paper needed). Avoid places people walk through.

### 2. Shopping and preparation

- **Sticks:** have them cut to exactly 1.00 m at a timber yard or carpenter, or cut PVC pipe yourself. Tape a bottle cap or a bead on top: the round shadow makes the tip much easier to find.
- **Plumb lines:** string and a heavy nut, from any hardware shop.
- **Paper:** flip-chart paper from stationery shops, or opened-out cement or flour sacks. Students need at least 2 m × 1.5 m per team, so tape sheets together.
- **Card:** collect cereal and biscuit boxes for a few weeks. Each team needs two 25 × 25 cm pieces for the dial plus scraps for the stand.
- **Long pencils or bamboo skewers** (skewers are sold in markets and supermarkets).
- **Oranges and a battery torch** for step 3. No mains lamps.
- **A map of Ethiopia with a scale bar**, from a bookshop or the school atlas.
- Print or copy the declination table (step 5) and the sundial correction table (step 4) on the board.

### 3. Make a reference north–south line

On session 2's day, run the equal-shadow method yourself on a separate stick, carefully, and mark the line in paint or chalk. Check it against a compass: in Addis, magnetic north is within a few degrees of true north, but stay away from metal gates, cars and reinforced concrete pillars, which deflect a compass. This line is your check for the **north-line** sign-off.

### 4. Arrange the partner city (for step 6, option A)

If you can, a week ahead, find a partner at least 300 km north or south: another school, a colleague, or a student's relative in Mekelle, Gondar, Bahir Dar, Hawassa or Arba Minch. Send them the instructions from step 5 (vertical 1.00 m stick, plumb line, level ground, readings every 2 minutes around noon). Agree a date. Their solar noon is at a slightly different clock time from Addis's (Mekelle about 3 minutes earlier, Hawassa about 1 minute later, Gondar and Bahir Dar about 5 minutes later). They should simply record the shortest shadow. Option B (given data) always works as a fallback.

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1, repeat at the start of every outdoor session)

- **Never look at the sun.** Not for a second, and not through sunglasses, film, a paper tube, a phone camera or a reflection in water or a mirror. Eye damage from the sun is painless and permanent. Everything in this module uses shadows.
- Watch for students trying to "check" the sun's position by squinting up. Stop them each time.
- Hats and water; shade between readings. Addis is at about 2,400 m, so ultraviolet light at midday is strong even when it feels cool.
- Sticks have caps on top and stay in the ground. Nobody walks across another team's plot.
- Compass points are used to make holes only into a rubber or folded card, pointing away from the hand.
- Everyone stays inside the compound.

## Step by step

### Step 1: Twelve o'clock at dawn (60 min)

**Answer key for the puzzle.**

1. Rule: international = Ethiopian + 6 hours (or − 6 hours). Morning/afternoon/night words say which half of the day.
2. Ethiopian 12 o'clock in the morning is 6:00 am: roughly sunrise.
3. 9:00 pm = 3 o'clock at night (ምሽት 3 ሰዓት); midnight = 6 o'clock at night (ሌሊት 6 ሰዓት).

Sunrise times in the table are real for Addis (EAT) and London (GMT in winter, BST in summer). Addis moves only 40 minutes across the year; London moves about 3 hours on the clock even with its summer clock change, and over 4 hours in sun time.

Don't explain the answer yet. Collect predictions. Common predictions: "the shadow disappears at noon" (only on about 14 April and 30 August), "the shadow points the same way all day".

Check every stick with the plumb line yourself. A stick leaning by 2° gives a latitude error of up to 2° later.

### Step 2: Follow the shadow (120 min)

Run a rota so each team has at least one person at its plot at every reading time. Readings every 30 minutes from 9:30 to 15:00, and every 10 minutes from 11:50 to 12:50 (shift that hour later from January to March, when solar noon is nearer 12:35).

**Expected shadow lengths for a 1.00 m stick in Addis** (useful to spot mistakes):

| EAT | 1 December | 15 January | 15 February |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9:30 | 122 cm | 139 cm | 128 cm |
| 10:30 | 84 cm | 93 cm | 81 cm |
| 11:30 | 64 cm | 68 cm | 53 cm |
| 12:30 | 60 cm | 59 cm | 41 cm |
| 13:30 | 72 cm | 66 cm | 47 cm |
| 14:30 | 101 cm | 89 cm | 71 cm |

**Predicted solar noon for Addis (EAT)** (read off the nearest date):

| Date | Solar noon | Date | Solar noon |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jan | 12:28 | 1 Jul | 12:28 |
| 15 Jan | 12:34 | 15 Jul | 12:31 |
| 1 Feb | 12:38 | 1 Aug | 12:32 |
| 15 Feb | 12:39 | 15 Aug | 12:30 |
| 1 Mar | 12:38 | 1 Sep | 12:25 |
| 15 Mar | 12:35 | 15 Sep | 12:20 |
| 1 Apr | 12:29 | 1 Oct | 12:15 |
| 15 Apr | 12:25 | 15 Oct | 12:11 |
| 1 May | 12:22 | 1 Nov | 12:09 |
| 15 May | 12:21 | 15 Nov | 12:10 |
| 1 Jun | 12:22 | 1 Dec | 12:14 |
| 15 Jun | 12:25 | 15 Dec | 12:20 |

These are for central Addis (38.74°E). A school 5 km east or west changes them by less than a minute.

**Where students get stuck.**

- Near noon the shadow length barely changes for 20–30 minutes, so the "shortest shadow" is hard to pin down from lengths alone. That's normal. Teach them to use the **symmetry** of the curve: pick two equal lengths either side and take the time halfway between. That is usually good to ±5 minutes. Students within 10 minutes of the prediction have done well.
- Morning and afternoon dots on the same circle: if a cloud hides the afternoon crossing, use another circle.
- Labelling east and west backwards: the **morning** dot is **west**.
- The noon shadow points north from about 30 August to 14 April and south from 14 April to 30 August. If you run this near those dates, the noon shadow is tiny and the shadow-tip method still works, but the plot looks strange: tell students to expect it.

**Question 3 answer.** EAT is set for 45°E. Addis is 6.3° west, so the sun is due south about 6.3 × 4 ≈ 25 minutes after it is due south at 45°E. The rest of the difference is the equation of time (step 4).

### Step 3: A spinning, tilted Earth (75 min)

The torch-and-orange model works best in a darkened room with one small torch. Make sure the skewer keeps pointing at the **same wall** as students walk around the "sun"; the usual mistake is to keep it tilted towards the torch all the way round, which gives permanent summer.

**Graph answers.** Range in Addis: about 12.6 − 11.6 = 1.0 h (true value about 1 h 3 min). London: 16.6 − 7.9 = 8.7 h (about 8 h 45 min).

**Back to the puzzle, answers.**

1. Sunrise at 4:43 am UK time is 1 h 17 min before 6:00. On a dawn clock it would be about **10:43 of the night**: the "first hour of the day" would start long after dawn.
2. North of overhead from about 14 April to 30 August; noon shadows point **south** then.
3. Mekelle's overhead days are around **27 April and 18 August**: **closer together**, because 13.5°N is nearer the Tropic of Cancer, so the sun crosses that latitude later on its way north and earlier on its way back.

**Questions to ask.** "Where on the orange does the torch shine straight down in June? In December?" "Is anywhere always 12 hours of day?" (The equator.) "Why don't we have a cold winter like London?" (Near the equator the noon sun is always high, and the day is always about 12 hours, so the sunlight each day changes much less. In Ethiopia the seasons are about rain, kiremt and bega, more than temperature.) Correct anyone who says seasons are caused by distance from the sun: Earth is closest to the sun in early January.

### Step 4: Build a sun clock (105 min)

**The geometry, so you can check teams' dials.** The pencil must point north and rise at 9° (Addis's latitude). The card is then tilted 9° from vertical, top edge leaning south. The noon line on both faces points straight down.

- North face (where the pencil's north end comes out): seen from the north, pm hours go round to the **left** of the noon line, am hours to the **right**: clockwise from 6 am to 6 pm.
- South face: seen from the south, pm hours go to the **right**, am to the **left**: anticlockwise.
- The sun lights the south face from about 23 September to 20 March, and the north face the rest of the year. For a module in the dry season, students will be reading the **south face**, standing south of the dial looking north.

Most mistakes are in the hour labelling. Get each team to check: "where is the sun now (east or west, by the shadows, not by looking)? Which side of the noon line should the shadow be?" Morning shadows fall on the **west** side.

The 15° marks must be drawn from the exact centre, and the pencil must be at right angles to the card. A tilted pencil makes the dial wrong by up to an hour near 6 am and 6 pm.

**Expected accuracy.** With careful setup, corrected readings agree with the phone to within 5–10 minutes. Errors over 20 minutes usually mean the dial isn't on the north–south line, or the correction was subtracted instead of added. Example: on 1 December at 11:00 EAT the sundial should read about 10:46 (sun time), and 10:46 + 14 min = 11:00.

**Horizontal sundial extension, answers.** For Addis, $\tan\theta = 0.156 \tan(15t)$: 1 h: 2.4°, 2 h: 5.2°, 3 h: 8.9°, 4 h: 15.2°, 5 h: 30.4°, 6 h: 90°. For London ($0.783$): 11.8°, 24.3°, 38.1°, 53.6°, 71.1°, 90°. In Addis the lines from 9 am to 3 pm are crowded into about 18° of the dial, so a horizontal dial is almost useless at midday. This is why equatorial dials suit Ethiopia.

### Step 5: How high is the sun? (90 min)

Start about 45 minutes before solar noon. While waiting, teams do the practice calculations from their step 2 data.

**Morning elevation rate.** Expect about 10–13° per hour between 9:30 and 10:30 (for example, 1 December: 39° at 9:30 to 50° at 10:30). It is less than 15° per hour because the sun's path across the sky is tilted, so only part of its 15° per hour motion is upwards, and it slows to zero as it reaches its highest point at noon.

**Expected noon shadows for a 1.00 m stick in Addis** and the latitude students should get:

| Date | Declination | Noon shadow | $z$ | Latitude $\delta + z$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Nov | −14.2° | 43 cm, north | 23.2° | 9.0° |
| 15 Nov | −18.3° | 52 cm, north | 27.3° | 9.0° |
| 1 Dec | −21.7° | 59 cm, north | 30.7° | 9.0° |
| 21 Dec | −23.4° | 64 cm, north | 32.5° | 9.0° |
| 15 Jan | −21.3° | 58 cm, north | 30.3° | 9.0° |
| 15 Feb | −13.0° | 40 cm, north | 22.0° | 9.0° |
| 1 Mar | −7.8° | 30 cm, north | 16.8° | 9.0° |

A good team gets within about 1° (about 110 km). Sources of error, in order of importance: a stick that isn't vertical; uneven ground; missing the true noon minimum; finding the middle of the fuzzy tip; using the wrong declination. Each 1° of error in $z$ or $\delta$ gives a 1° error in latitude. A 1 cm error in a 60 cm shadow changes $z$ by about 0.4°.

**Record question 3.** On 14 April, the sun is almost overhead: a 1.00 m stick casts a noon shadow of under 1 cm. On 21 December: $\tan(9.0 + 23.4) = \tan 32.4° \approx 0.64$, so about 64 cm, pointing north.

**Fair-test check.** Make sure all three readers measured independently, and that the table has a mean and range. A range bigger than 2 cm means the method needs tightening (tip not clear, stick moved).

### Step 6: Measure the Earth (90 min)

**Option B answers** (21 December; the data are calculated from the cities' true latitudes, so they work out well):

| Pair | $z_{\text{south}}$ | $z_{\text{north}}$ | Difference | Distance | Circumference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hawassa–Mekelle | 30.50° | 36.94° | 6.44° | 716 km | about 40,000 km |
| Addis–Mekelle | 32.46° | 36.94° | 4.48° | 497 km | about 39,900 km |
| Hawassa–Addis | 30.50° | 32.46° | 1.96° | 219 km | about 40,200 km |

The Addis–Mekelle distance is 716 − 219 = 497 km. Rounding $z$ differently moves answers by a few hundred km; anything from 39,500 to 40,500 km is right.

**Puzzling question 1.** Changing Hawassa's shadow to 0.591 m makes its $z$ 30.59°, the difference 1.87°, and the circumference about **42,100 km**: a 2 mm error makes a 5% error, because the angle difference between close cities is small. Cities far apart give a bigger angle and a more reliable answer.

**Option A (real partner data).** With shadows good to ±0.5 cm, each $z$ is good to about ±0.3°, so the difference is uncertain by up to ±0.6°. For Addis–Mekelle (4.5°) that's about ±13%, so an answer between 35,000 and 46,000 km is a genuine success. Map distances also carry error: students must take the **north–south** component only, not the road distance (Addis to Mekelle by road is well over 700 km).

**Common confusions.** Students add the angles instead of subtracting; they use the road distance; they think the sun's rays spread out from a nearby sun (show that the sun is about 150 million km away, so rays reaching Ethiopia are parallel to within a tiny fraction of a degree).

**Historical note.** Eratosthenes' result was 252,000 stadia. We don't know exactly how long his stadion was, so his accuracy is debated; it was somewhere between very good and within about 15%.

### Step 7: Sun clock day (60 min)

Run the challenge with three "Read!" calls 15–20 minutes apart. Do the sign-off conversations with one team while the others prepare.

Tell students that the home sundial needs a sunny spot open to both the morning and afternoon sun, aimed north; a north-facing window only gets sun from April to August.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Shadow tip too fuzzy | Use the bottle cap's round shadow and mark its centre. Thin sticks give sharper shadows than thick ones. |
| Equal-shadow lines disagree | The stick moved, or the ground isn't level. Re-plumb and use the circles closest to noon, which change fastest in direction. |
| Solar noon from the graph is 20+ minutes out | Too few points near noon; use the symmetry method. Check the phone shows network time. |
| Sundial is an hour out | Labels on the wrong side, or reading the unlit face. |
| Sundial is 15–40 minutes out | Correction not applied, or subtracted. Dial not on the north–south line. |
| Sundial shadow falls off the card | It is near 6 am/6 pm (the shadow runs along the card), or near an equinox. |
| Latitude comes out near −30° or 50° | Wrong sign: check which way the noon shadow pointed, and the sign of the declination. |
| Calculator gives strange angles | It is in radians (RAD) mode. Switch to DEG. |

## Judging the sign-off items

- **north-line:** compare the team's north–south line with your reference line or a compass away from metal. Within about 5° passes. They must also explain which end is north and why the morning mark is west.
- **shadow-graph:** labelled axes with units, a smooth curve (not dot-to-dot), solar noon read off it, and a comparison with the predicted time from the table above. Within 10 minutes is good; within 20 minutes passes if they can say why they were out.
- **sundial:** pencil at right angles to the card and tilted about 9° from horizontal (check with a protractor), pointing north along the line, hours on the correct sides, Ethiopian hours marked. They read it in both systems and apply the correction to get within about 10 minutes of the phone. If cloud stops the final check, accept their step 4 readings.
- **latitude:** working shows $s$, $h$, $z = \tan^{-1}(s/h)$, the declination for the date, the correct rule for the shadow direction, and an answer between 7.5° and 10.5°N.
- **circumference:** a labelled diagram with parallel rays, the formula, working for at least one pair, a percentage difference from 40,000 km, and at least two real sources of error (not just "human error").
- **explains:** must link dawn clocks to the small change in day length near the equator, and say the sun is overhead when its declination equals 9°N, which happens once on the way north (about 14 April) and once on the way back (about 30 August). Accept "mid-April and the end of August".
- **safety:** if a student looked at the sun after a reminder, talk with them before signing.

## Notes for experts reviewing this draft

- Astronomical numbers (declination, equation of time, solar noon, sunrise and day length) were calculated with the standard NOAA approximation for Addis Ababa (9.03°N, 38.74°E) and are good to about a minute and 0.3°; they vary slightly from year to year.
- The commonly quoted dates for the overhead sun in Addis are sometimes given as "late April and mid-August"; those are correct for Mekelle (13.5°N), not Addis. For 9.0°N the dates are about 14 April and 30 August.
- Curriculum tags for **Earth, Sun and time** (especially "The Earth in space", grade 7) need checking against the current national curriculum.
- Amharic time phrases should be checked by a native speaker for the most natural wording.
