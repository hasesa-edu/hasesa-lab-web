A stick in the ground is one of the oldest scientific instruments in the world. Its shadow tells you the time, the direction of north, how far you are from the equator, and even how big the Earth is. In ten hours you'll get all four answers yourself, from a 1 m stick and some careful measuring.

1. **Twelve o'clock at dawn** (60 min). Why does Ethiopian time start at dawn? Set up your stick and make predictions.
2. **Follow the shadow** (120 min). Mark the shadow's tip through a whole day. Find true north and the moment of solar noon.
3. **A spinning, tilted Earth** (75 min). Model Earth with an orange and a torch, and find out why days in Ethiopia are always about 12 hours long.
4. **Build a sun clock** (105 min). Make an equatorial sundial that reads both Ethiopian and international time, and correct it to the clock.
5. **How high is the sun?** (90 min). Use shadows and the tangent ratio to measure the sun's height, and from it the latitude of Addis.
6. **Measure the Earth** (90 min). Repeat Eratosthenes' famous experiment with noon shadows from two Ethiopian cities.
7. **Sun clock day** (60 min). Show your sundial and your results, and explain them to the guide.

By the end you'll understand Earth's rotation and tilt, latitude and longitude, time zones and Ethiopian time, and you'll have used trigonometry to measure a whole planet.
