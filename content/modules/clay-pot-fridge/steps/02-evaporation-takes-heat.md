## Do: set up the bottles first (10 minutes)

The bottles take time, so start them now. Your team needs three identical 0.5 L bottles, filled to the top with water from the same jerrycan, so they start at the same temperature.

1. **Bottle A:** no cloth.
2. **Bottle B:** wrapped in a **dry** cotton cloth, held with a rubber band.
3. **Bottle C:** wrapped in the same size of cloth, soaked in water and squeezed so it doesn't drip.

Put a thermometer in each bottle and record the starting temperatures. Weigh bottle C, with its wet cloth, on the scale and record the mass.

Stand all three side by side in the shade where the air moves (an open window or doorway). Leave them for 30 minutes.

## Do: wet and dry thermometers (15 minutes)

Take two identical thermometers. Leave one bare (the **dry bulb**). Wrap the bulb of the other in a small strip of cotton cloth, dipped in water at room temperature (the **wet bulb**).

Read both every minute for 10 minutes, fanning them gently with a piece of card. Record:

| Time (min) | Dry (°C) | Wet (°C) | Difference (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | | | |
| 1 | | | |
| … | | | |

The water on the wet bulb started at room temperature. So where did it get colder from?

Try this too: put a drop of water on the back of your hand and blow across it. Then blow across a dry patch of skin. What do you feel?

## Notice something puzzling

Nothing in this experiment is cold. There's no ice and no fridge. All the water started at room temperature. Yet the wet thermometer went **below** room temperature, and stayed there. What is taking the energy out of it?

## Theory: evaporation and humidity

Read **Evaporation and humidity**, the sections *What evaporation is* and *Why evaporation cools*. You'll learn:

- that evaporation is the fastest particles escaping from the surface,
- why the water left behind gets colder,
- that evaporating 1 g of water takes about 2,400 J, the **latent heat of vaporisation**,
- how much that can cool something, using $\text{energy} = m \times L$.

## Do it better: the energy sum (25 minutes)

After 30 minutes, read the three bottle thermometers. Weigh bottle C again.

1. How much colder is bottle C than bottle A? Than bottle B? What does bottle B tell you that bottle C alone could not?
2. How many grams of water evaporated from bottle C's cloth? (Start mass − end mass.)
3. Energy carried away by that water: $E = m \times 2400$ J.
4. Energy the 500 g of water in the bottle lost as it cooled: $E = 500 \times 4.2 \times \text{temperature drop}$.

Compare your two energies. You'll probably find that the evaporation took away **more** energy than the bottle lost. Where did the extra energy come from? (Hint: think about the air around the bottle, and *Heat transfer*.)
