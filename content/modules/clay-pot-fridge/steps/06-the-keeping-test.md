## The question

A fridge is only useful if food lasts longer in it. Does your zeer really keep food fresh for longer than a table in the room, and if so, by how much?

**Remember the rule: nothing from this test is eaten or drunk.** Food that looks and smells fine can still carry enough bacteria to make you ill, and you can't see them.

## Set up (20 minutes)

Make two identical sets of food:

| Food | Set for the zeer | Set for the room |
|---|---|---|
| Tomatoes | 3, as alike as possible, on a small plate | 3 more from the same batch, on a plate |
| Gomen (or other leafy greens) | a handful, about 100 g, weighed | the same mass, from the same bunch |
| Milk | 100 mL in a clean jar, lid on loosely | 100 mL from the same milk, in an identical jar |

1. Number each tomato with a marker. Weigh each tomato and each bundle of greens. Record the masses.
2. Pour the milk into both jars at the same time. Smell each one now, so you know what "fresh" smells like.
3. Put one set in the inner pot of your zeer. Make sure nothing touches the wet sand or the cloth lid. Put the other set on a table in the room, in the shade, next to your outside thermometer.
4. If your guide sets up a **warm place** too (for example a closed cardboard box near a sunny window), add a third set there, with its own thermometer.

Why is it important that both sets come from the same batch and start on the same day? Which variable are you changing, and which are you keeping the same?

## Check every day (5 minutes a day, for 5 days)

At the same time each day:

1. Read the temperature inside the zeer and in the room.
2. Weigh each tomato and each bundle of greens.
3. Give each food a score using the scale below. Look closely; touch the tomatoes gently; lift the milk lid a little and smell from a short distance, then close it.
4. Top up the zeer's sand with water.

| Score | Tomatoes and greens | Milk |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | fresh, firm, bright | smells fresh, runs like water |
| 1 | slightly soft or slightly wilted | slightly sour smell |
| 2 | soft or wrinkled; greens limp and yellowing | sour smell, a little thicker (like irgo) |
| 3 | very soft, leaking, or dark patches | thick and lumpy, whey separating |
| 4 | mould or rotting | mould, or a bad (not just sour) smell |

The **keeping time** is the number of days until a food first reaches a score of 2.

Wash your hands with soap after every check.

## Theory: microbes

Read **Microbes**, especially *How fast they grow* and *What microbes need, and what stops them*. You'll learn:

- what bacteria and moulds are, and why some are helpful (irgo, injera) and some cause disease,
- how bacteria multiply by doubling, $N = N_0 \times 2^n$,
- why cold slows them down but doesn't kill them.

## Do the maths (10 minutes)

Milk has a few thousand bacteria in every millilitre even when it's fresh. Suppose the bacteria in a jar double every **1 hour** in the warm room, but only every **2 hours** in the cooler zeer. Starting with 1,000 bacteria per millilitre:

1. How many doublings happen in 12 hours in the room? In the zeer?
2. How many bacteria per millilitre after 12 hours in each place? Use $N = N_0 \times 2^n$.
3. How many times more bacteria are in the room jar?

A rough rule for many food microbes: for every 10 °C cooler, they grow about **half as fast**. Use your zeer's mean difference from step 5 to say what that means for your food.

## Record (20 minutes, on the last day)

1. A results table for each food: day, temperature in each place, mass and score.
2. For the tomatoes and greens, work out the **percentage of mass lost** by the last day:
$$\text{percentage lost} = \frac{\text{start mass} - \text{end mass}}{\text{start mass}} \times 100$$
Vegetables lose water as they wilt. Did the zeer's damp, cool air help?
3. Draw a bar chart of **keeping time** for each food, zeer and room side by side (and the warm place, if you had one).
4. Answer: how many days longer did each food last in the zeer? Was the difference the same for every food? Which food did the zeer help most?

Then your guide collects every jar and plate. The food is sealed in a bag and thrown away, or buried. Nothing goes back to a kitchen.
