# Clay Pot Fridge: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 8 hours of contact time for up to 16 students in teams of three or four, plus short daily checks over one week. Run it in **bega** (October to January) or the dry part of belg if you can: the zeer needs dry air. In kiremt it cools by only a degree or two, which is a useful lesson but a disappointing build.

## Before the module

### 1. Shopping

- **Clay pots.** Two per team, plus one demo set and two spares (they crack). Potters at Kechene sell direct; Merkato has clay-pot sellers; roadside plant nurseries sell terracotta flower pots in nested sizes, which are the easiest way to get a good fit. The outer pot must be **unglazed and unpainted**: drip water on it and check it soaks in. A good team size is an outer pot of about 30–35 cm across and an inner pot 6–10 cm narrower. The inner pot can be glazed or even a metal pot if clay is short; only the outer one must breathe.
- **Sand (ashewa).** About 5 kg per team. Rinse it in a bucket until the water runs fairly clear; muddy sand clogs the clay.
- **Cotton cloth (abujedid).** One lid per team plus strips for wet bulbs and the cloths for step 3 (six squares of about 20 × 20 cm per team, all cut the same).
- **Thermometers.** At least two per team, ideally three (inside, outside, and a wet bulb). Alcohol glass thermometers from lab-supply shops around Piassa and Arat Kilo, or cheap digital indoor/outdoor thermometers with a wire probe (the probe goes inside the zeer, under the lid edge, so students can read it without opening the pot). No mercury thermometers.
- **Kitchen scales** reading to 1 g, one per two teams at least.
- **Food for step 6,** bought on the day of setup: ripe (fully red) tomatoes from one seller, gomen from one bunch, and fresh milk from one container. Ripe tomatoes show differences within a week; hard green ones won't.
- Clean jam jars with lids, plates, paper bags, markers, plastic bags for disposal, soap.

### 2. Match the thermometers (30 minutes, the week before)

The differences in this module are only a few degrees, so thermometer errors matter. Put every thermometer in the same bucket of water at room temperature, stir, wait five minutes and read them all. Label each with its error (for example "+0.5"). Pair thermometers that agree within 0.5 °C for each team's inside/outside pair, or teach teams to correct their readings. Retire any that are more than 1 °C off.

### 3. The demo set (two or three days before session 1)

- **Fill an ensira** (or an unglazed clay jar) and an identical volume in a plastic bottle the day before session 1, side by side in the room.
- **Build the demo zeer** exactly as in step 4, two days ahead, in a shady, breezy spot near the classroom. Keep the sand wet. On the morning of session 1, put a thermometer inside and a matched one hanging in the shade beside it. By mid-morning in bega it should be 3–6 °C cooler inside.
- **Soak the students' pots** in water on the morning of session 3 if you can.

### 4. Places

Find shady, airy spots for every team's zeer, off the ground (two bricks are enough), safe from football and goats, and visible from a classroom window if possible. Find a "warm place" for the optional third food set in step 6: a closed cardboard box near a sunny window works, reaching 25–35 °C in the afternoon. Put a thermometer in it.

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1, repeat when the food goes in)

- Nothing from the keeping test is eaten or drunk. Not a lick, not a sip. Explain why: harmful bacteria can't be seen or smelled, and the milk will be grown on purpose.
- Milk is smelled from a short distance with the lid just lifted. No deep sniffing, no opening jars wide, and never near the face once it is lumpy or mouldy.
- Soap and water after every check, every time. Have a basin, soap and a jerrycan with a tap ready by the zeers.
- Clay pots: two hands, one pot per person, fill them where they stand. You clear up any broken pottery.
- Glass thermometers: hold by the top, don't shake. If one breaks, everyone steps back; you sweep up the glass. Alcohol thermometers are not poisonous, but the glass cuts.
- Outdoor logging: shade, hats, water.
- No mains electricity: fanning is done with cardboard.
- **Disposal at the end:** you collect all jars and plates. Close the milk jars, put everything into a strong plastic bag, tie it, and put it in the bin or bury it at least 30 cm deep. Jars that will be reused are emptied into a toilet or pit latrine, then washed with hot soapy water by you. Students with a milk allergy can do the vegetable checks only.

## Step by step

### Step 1: The pot that sweats (45 min)

The outer zeer pot should feel damp and noticeably cool; the ensira water a little cooler than the bottle water (typically 2–5 °C in bega). Read the demo thermometers out loud and write both on the board. The wet-cloth bottle by the window will be clearly cooler to the touch after 30–40 minutes. Don't explain anything yet. Collect the tomato guesses: most students guess too high.

### Step 2: Evaporation takes heat (75 min)

**Expected results (Addis, bega, indoors, about 20 °C and 40–50% humidity):**

- Wet bulb falls fast for 2–3 minutes, then levels off. Final difference with fanning: about 5–8 °C. In kiremt, 1–3 °C.
- Bottles after 30 minutes in a breezy window: A and B within 0.5 °C of each other; C 2–4 °C colder than A.
- Water lost from C's cloth: about 5–15 g.
- **Energy sum, a typical example:** 10 g evaporated × 2,400 J/g = 24,000 J. The bottle's 500 g of water cooled by 3 °C: 500 × 4.2 × 3 = 6,300 J. Evaporation took away nearly four times as much as the bottle lost. **The rest came from the air**: once the cloth is colder than the air, heat flows into it from the room. This is the key idea: a zeer is a fight between evaporation pulling heat out and the warm surroundings pushing heat in.
- Bottle B (dry cloth) is the control: it shows the cloth itself doesn't cool; only the water does.

**Where students get stuck:** many think the water "is cold" because it is wet. Ask: what temperature was the water when you put it on? Then why is it colder now? Others think evaporation needs boiling; point to washing drying on a line.

**Questions to ask:** Why do you feel cold stepping out of a shower? Why does blowing on hot shiro cool it? What would happen to the wet bulb if the air were already full of water vapour?

### Step 3: What speeds it up? (90 min)

**Expected results (20 × 20 cm cloths, 40 g of water, 30 minutes, indoors in bega):**

| Test | Typical mean loss | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Still cloth, spread | 6–10 g | baseline |
| Fanned | 12–25 g | about 2–3 times faster |
| Folded into a tight square | 2–5 g | about half or less |
| Sealed in a bag | 0–2 g | nearly stopped: the air in the bag becomes saturated |

With a 1 g scale, ranges of 1–3 g are normal; that's why repeats matter. If a team's means differ by less than their ranges, praise them for noticing and suggest a longer time or bigger cloths.

**Weighing the bag:** students must take the cloth out to weigh it. Weighing the sealed bag shows no loss at all, because the vapour is still inside the bag. If a team does this by mistake, it's a great teaching moment.

**Answers for placing the zeer:** shade (sunlight heats the pot by radiation, and that heat has to be removed by more evaporation); a breeze (from the wind test); a dry place, away from cooking steam and washing (from the humidity test); a bigger outer pot gives more evaporating surface (from the surface test), though it also lets more heat in.

### Step 4: Build the zeer (75 min)

Common faults: a glazed or painted outer pot (no sweating at all); a gap under 2 cm (too little sand and water); sand too dry; the lid cloth not touching the wet sand, so it dries out; the inner pot rim above the outer one, so the lid sags. A team zeer of the size above takes about 2–4 L of water to soak the sand the first time and then roughly 0.5–1.5 L a day in Addis in bega. Remind teams to top up daily.

### Step 5: A day in the pot (75 min plus readings)

**Expected results (Addis, bega, shady and breezy spot):**

- Outside: about 14–16 °C at 08:00, peaking at 22–25 °C around 13:00–15:00.
- Inside: close to outside early in the morning (difference 1–3 °C), widening to 4–7 °C at the hottest time. The inside peak usually comes 30–60 minutes **later** than the outside peak, because the wet sand and pots take time to warm.
- Wet bulb at the hottest time: about 12–14 °C.
- Mean difference over the day: about 3–5 °C.
- **Score:** for example outside 24 °C, inside 18 °C, wet bulb 13 °C: (24 − 18) ÷ (24 − 13) = 6 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.55. Scores of 0.4–0.7 are typical. Above 0.8 is suspicious: check that the inside thermometer isn't touching the wet sand and that the outside one isn't in the sun.

**Predictions with a score of 0.6:**

- Semera: 38 − 0.6 × (38 − 24) = 38 − 8.4 ≈ 29.6 °C, so about 8 °C of cooling.
- Dire Dawa: 33 − 0.6 × (33 − 21) = 33 − 7.2 ≈ 25.8 °C, about 7 °C of cooling.
- Addis in kiremt: 17 − 0.6 × (17 − 15) = 17 − 1.2 ≈ 15.8 °C, about 1 °C of cooling.

Students should see that the zeer cools most where it's most needed: hot, dry lowlands where food spoils fastest. In Afar, 30 °C inside still isn't cold, but it's far better than 38 °C. (Practical zeer projects in Sudan, northern Nigeria and elsewhere report food lasting several times longer.) Note that the Afar figures are for dry periods; in the more humid summer months the cooling there is much smaller.

**Where students get stuck:** plotting two lines on one graph with a key; choosing a sensible scale (start the temperature axis near 10 °C, not at 0, and say so). Remind them that time on the x-axis must be evenly spaced even if a reading was missed.

### Step 6: The keeping test (75 min across the week)

**Expected results (Addis, bega; room about 18–22 °C, zeer about 4–6 °C cooler and much more humid):**

| Food | Keeping time, room | Keeping time, zeer | Mass lost by day 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ripe tomatoes | 4–6 days | 6–10 days (may not reach score 2 within the test) | room 3–6%, zeer 1–3% |
| Gomen | 1–2 days | 3–5 days | room 25–50%, zeer 5–15% |
| Raw milk | about 1–2 days | about 2–3 days | — |
| Pasteurised milk (opened) | about 2–3 days | about 4–5 days | — |
| Warm place (if used) | everything fastest; milk often sour within a day | | |

If a food hasn't reached score 2 by the last day, record "more than 5 days" and say so in the bar chart with an arrow. The greens show the clearest difference, and most of it is **water loss**: the zeer's air is cool and humid, so leaves lose less water. That's a good discussion: the zeer helps two ways, by slowing microbes (cooler) and by slowing wilting (damper).

**The maths:** room: 12 ÷ 1 = 12 doublings, 1,000 × 2¹² = 4,096,000 per mL. Zeer: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 doublings, 1,000 × 2⁶ = 64,000 per mL. The room jar has 4,096,000 ÷ 64,000 = **64 times** more. Halving the growth rate doesn't halve the bacteria; it square-roots them, because growth is exponential. For the "10 °C" rule: a zeer that is 5 °C cooler slows growth to roughly 0.7 of the room rate, so food lasts roughly 1.4 times as long. Treat this only as a rough rule; it varies a lot between microbes.

**Where students get stuck:** scoring honestly. Two students should score each food independently and agree. Remind them that irgo is milk soured on purpose by helpful bacteria, but uncontrolled sour milk can also carry harmful ones; that's why we never taste.

### Step 7: Pot report (45 min)

A good recommendation chooses the Afar lowlands or Dire Dawa in the dry season, uses the team's own score to predict cooling there, gives practical rules (shade, breeze, off the ground, wet the sand once or twice a day, keep vegetables and milk in it, not wet cloths touching food), and warns that a zeer slows spoilage but doesn't stop it or make bad food safe, and that it is far less cold than an electric fridge (about 4 °C). Some students will point out that in Afar water is scarce; give credit for that.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Outer pot never darkens or feels damp | Glazed or painted, or fired very hard. Swap it. |
| Zeer only 1 °C cooler | Humid day or still air; sand drying out; zeer in the sun; inside thermometer reading the room because the lid is open. Check each in turn. |
| Inside colder than the wet bulb | Thermometer touching wet sand or cloth, or the thermometers don't match. Recheck the offsets. |
| Water pools in the sand | Drainage hole not plugged; too much water. Stand it on a tray. |
| Mould on the sand or cloth | Normal after a week. Wash the cloth; rinse or replace the sand between groups. |
| Scale reading jumps | Wind on the scale pan; weigh indoors, out of the draught. |

## Judging the sign-off items

- **zeer-cools:** check the build (unglazed outer pot, even wet sand all round, wet lid touching the sand) and look at their log. At least 3 °C at the hottest time on a dry day. If the week was humid or rainy, accept 1–2 °C with your note, as long as the build is right and the team explains why.
- **explains-cooling:** must mention the fastest particles escaping and taking energy away, so what's left is colder, and heat then flowing from the food into the colder sand. "Water is cold" or "the clay keeps it cold" is not enough.
- **fair-test:** one variable changed; at least four controlled variables listed and really kept the same (same starting water in each cloth); three repeats; mean and range calculated correctly.
- **day-graph:** both lines on one graph, axes labelled with units, a key, evenly spaced time axis; biggest and mean differences correct from their own data.
- **keeping-test:** same-batch food in both places, daily scores and masses recorded, percentage mass loss calculated, a bar chart, and a conclusion that links the result to temperature slowing microbe growth (and, for greens, less water loss).
- **recommendation:** a clear choice backed by at least three of their own numbers and by humidity; includes the warning about what a zeer can't do.
- **safety:** never tasted, washed hands every check without reminders, followed disposal. Discuss any lapse before signing.
