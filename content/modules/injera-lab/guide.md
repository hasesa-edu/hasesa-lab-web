# Injera Lab: guide's notes

For the guide only. About 10 hours, best as five sessions of about two hours, for up to 16 students in teams of four. Several batters must be **started days ahead**; the calendar below is the hardest part of running this module. You don't need to be an expert injera maker, but it helps to practise one batch at home first, or to work with someone who makes injera every week.

---

## Calendar

| When | What you do |
|---|---|
| 4 days before step 1 | Mix the four hook batters: one jar each on days −3, −2, −1 and the morning of step 1. Same recipe for all (below). |
| 4 days before step 4 | Start the pH-series batter. Each day, put 50 mL in a labelled jar in the fridge (day 0, 1, 2, 3, 4). |
| After step 4 | Collect the teams' bake-test plans. |
| 1–3 days before step 6 | Mix each team's two batters at the right times so both are ready on baking day (for example, the 3-day batter three days before, the 1-day batter one day before). |
| Day of step 6 | Heat the mitad 15 minutes before; do a test injera. |

**Basic batter recipe (per 1 L jar):** 250 g teff flour, about 400 mL water, 2 tablespoons of ersho. Stir to a thick, smooth batter. Mark the level on the jar. Cover loosely (a cloth or a lid laid on top, not screwed down) and keep at room temperature. After a day or two, a layer of yellowish liquid (new ersho) forms on top. On baking day, thin with water to the consistency of thin pancake batter.

**Absit (if a team tests it):** on baking day, take about 100 mL of fermented batter, stir it into 300 mL of boiling water in a pan, and stir over heat until it thickens (2–3 minutes). Cool to warm and stir it back into the batter. Wait 1–2 hours before baking: the batter rises again.

## Shopping and preparation

| Item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Teff flour | About 3 kg. White or brown both work; use the same for everything. |
| Ersho | A cup from a household or injera bakery. Keep it in the fridge. |
| Baker's yeast | Fresh packets. |
| Bikil | Ground malted barley. If you can only find whole grains, grind them in a coffee grinder. |
| Limewater | From a lab supplier, or make it: stir a teaspoon of builders' lime (calcium hydroxide) into 1 L of water, let it settle overnight, and pour off the clear liquid. Label it; it's irritating to eyes, so you handle it. |
| Red cabbage | Make the indicator on the day; it keeps a day in the fridge. |
| Electric mitad | Borrow one if the school has none. Check the plug and cable. |

## Safety briefing (step 1; again at step 6)

- You run the mitad. Students pour only with you beside them; long sleeves rolled up, hair tied back, faces away from steam.
- Hot water: you pour. Warm baths only, near students.
- Iodine and limewater: gloves and glasses.
- Food hygiene. Separate "eating" batter (made in clean food containers) from "experiment" batter. Tasting only from eating batter and finished injera, with clean hands. Nobody tastes anything from the bottles, indicators or test solutions.
- Ask about allergies: bikil and wheat contain gluten.

## Session plans

### Step 1: The bubbling bucket (45 min)

Day 0 batter: flat, no bubbles, smells of flour. Day 1: a few bubbles, slight sour smell. Days 2–3: risen, bubbly, clear sour smell, a liquid layer on top. Students can often hear faint fizzing in active batter. Typical eye counts: 15–40 per 4 cm², depending on the injera.

### Step 2: Alive or not? (75 min)

**Expected:** A grows fast (visible in 10–15 minutes). B grows a little (yeast packets contain a small amount of food, and yeast has some stored food). C and E: nothing. D: very slow. The batter bottle grows slowly; this shows living microbes are in the batter too.

Limewater: pinch the balloon neck, attach a straw, and squeeze the gas slowly through 10 mL of limewater. It turns milky.

### Step 3: Measure the gas (90 min)

Practise the gas collector beforehand; leaks around the tube are the main problem (seal with modelling clay or tape). The 5-minute wait matters because warming air in the bottle gives a false start.

**Expected class graph:** rate rises from 10 °C to a peak around 35–40 °C, then falls; at 50 °C yeast usually starts and then slows quickly; above 55–60 °C it stops. Some teams' numbers will be noisy; use the class average.

### Step 4: The sour side (75 min)

Red cabbage indicator: red/pink (pH 2–3), pink-violet (4–5), purple (6–7), blue (8), green (9–11), yellow (12+). **Expected batter pH:** day 0 about 6 (purple), falling to about 4 by days 3–4 (pink-red). Finished injera is about 4. Day 0 to day 4 is about a hundredfold increase in acidity.

Collect bake-test plans at the end. Check that each changes only one factor.

### Step 5: Enzymes at work (90 min)

Make the starch solution beforehand (it must be cooled). Make the malt water fresh. Test the dilution yourself: aim for the starch to disappear in about 3–6 minutes at room temperature. If it's faster, dilute the malt water; if slower, use more bikil.

**Expected:** 40 °C fastest (often under 3 minutes), 25 °C slower, 10 °C much slower, boiled malt never (stays blue-black). Malt amylase works well up to about 60–65 °C, so a 40 °C bath is safely below its optimum; that's fine.

**Absit question:** there is no settled single answer. Reasonable ideas: cooking gelatinises starch so enzymes can break it down faster, giving the microbes a new burst of sugar (a second rise); it thickens the batter so it holds the gas; it changes texture. Accept any idea backed by reasoning.

### Step 6: Bake the test (120 min)

Keep the mitad at a steady heat. Bake a test injera first and discard it. Use the same cup to pour every time. Typical observations: 1-day batter gives fewer eyes and a milder taste; 3-day batter more eyes and more sour; batter without ersho starts much more slowly; absit batter often gives more even eyes.

Run the blind taste test with pieces only from injera made from eating batter in clean containers.

### Step 7: Report (75 min)

Sign-off conversations while teams finish flowcharts.

## Judging the sign-off items

- **alive:** uses the boiled-yeast and no-yeast controls as evidence; names yeasts and lactic acid bacteria.
- **rate-graph:** rate (not volume) on the y-axis, with units; describes the peak and the fall.
- **ph-track:** three or more days; explains the fall by lactic acid bacteria.
- **enzyme-test:** amylase breaks starch into sugar; boiling denatures it.
- **bake-test:** one factor changed, measurements (not just opinions), and a fair comparison.
- **equations:** glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (gas, eyes); glucose → lactic acid (sourness).
