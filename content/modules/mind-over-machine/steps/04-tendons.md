## Do: open a chicken wing

**Safety first:** gloves on, work on a tray, keep the chicken away from your face and any food. Never eat it. Your guide will show you the cleaning-up routine.

A chicken wing is built like your arm: an "upper arm" (the drumette), a "forearm" (the flat middle part, with **two** bones — like your radius and ulna) and a "hand" (the tip).

1. Before cutting, move the wing. Which joints bend? Which way? Compare with your own elbow and wrist.
2. With scissors, carefully cut the skin along the length of the wing and peel it back. You will see pink **muscle** wrapped in thin see-through skin.
3. Look near the joints: find shiny white cords. These are **tendons**.
4. Hold the wing still and **gently pull one tendon** with tweezers or your gloved fingers. What moves? Pull a tendon on the other side. What moves now?
5. Find a muscle and follow it to its tendon, and the tendon to where it joins the bone.
6. At a joint, look for the tough bands joining bone to bone (ligaments) and the smooth white caps on the bone ends (cartilage).

Sketch what you see and label: muscle, tendon, bone, joint. Then clean up exactly as the guide shows you and wash your hands.

## Notice

- Pulling a tendon near the joint made the tip move a **long** way. Why?
- You found that one tendon bends the joint and another straightens it. Why does the wing need **two**?
- Feel your forearm as you wiggle your fingers. Your finger muscles are in your **forearm**, pulling long tendons through your wrist. That is why the guide's stickers were on the forearm!

## Just enough theory

Read *Muscles, tendons and levers*. Key ideas:

- Muscles can only **pull**, so they work in **antagonistic pairs** (flexors close, extensors open).
- Inside a muscle, actin and myosin filaments **slide** past each other and the muscle shortens.
- A stronger grip = **more motor units** switched on, firing faster.
- Bones are **levers**: $F_\text{muscle} \times d_\text{muscle} = F_\text{load} \times d_\text{load}$.

## Build: a cardboard hand with tendons

This hand becomes the body of your robot claw. Keep it simple: **two or three fingers** facing a **thumb** grab better than five.

1. Trace your hand on stiff cardboard and cut it out (or draw a claw shape: a palm about 8 cm wide, fingers about 2 cm wide and 8 cm long).
2. On each finger, draw lines where your knuckles would be (2 or 3 joints). **Fold** each line forwards and back several times so it bends easily.
3. Cut drinking straws into short pieces (1–2 cm). Glue them on the **palm side** of each finger segment, in a line, with gaps at each fold. These are the tendon sheaths — like the tunnels tendons run through in your hand.
4. Thread a string through the straws of each finger and **tie or tape it firmly to the fingertip**. Run all strings down through a straw on the palm to the wrist.
5. Pull a string: the finger should curl. Let go: does it straighten? If not, add a **rubber band** on the back of each finger as an "extensor" that pulls it open again.
6. Tape the wrist end of the hand to a cardboard base or a stiff piece of card, leaving room for a servo near the wrist.

## Think

1. Which part of your cardboard hand is the muscle? The tendon? The bone? The joint? (Hint: right now **you** are the muscle.)
2. If the string is tied 1 cm from a joint and the fingertip is 6 cm from it, how far does the tip move compared with how far you pull the string?
3. Solve: you hold a 2 kg bag of sugar 32 cm from your elbow; your biceps attaches 4 cm from the elbow. How hard must the biceps pull? Why is the body "designed" this way?
