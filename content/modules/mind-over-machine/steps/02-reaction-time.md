## Do: the ruler drop

Work in pairs: a **dropper** and a **catcher**.

1. The catcher rests their forearm on the edge of a table, hand hanging over, thumb and finger open about 3 cm apart.
2. The dropper holds a 30 cm ruler vertically, with the **0 cm mark exactly level** with the top of the catcher's thumb.
3. Without warning, the dropper lets go. The catcher pinches it as fast as possible.
4. Read the mark at the top of the thumb. That is the distance $d$ the ruler fell.
5. Do **5 trials**. Swap roles.

Make a table in your notebook: trial, distance (cm). Find your **average**.

## Notice

The ruler fell some distance before you caught it. That means time passed between "it moves!" and "fingers close". Your body is not instant.

**But how much time?** You measured a distance, not a time. A ruler doesn't come with a stopwatch... or does it?

## Just enough theory

Read the unit *Free fall and square roots*. The key idea: a dropped object falls a known distance in a known time,

$$d = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}}, \qquad g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$$

The unit *Rearranging formulas* shows how we turned the first formula into the second.

**Worked check:** caught at 20 cm → $d = 0.20$ m → $t = \sqrt{0.40 / 9.8} \approx 0.20$ s.

Now calculate **your** reaction time from your average distance. Remember to convert cm to m!

## Do it better: add a distraction

Repeat 5 trials, but this time the catcher must **count backwards from 100 in sevens** out loud (100, 93, 86, …) while waiting. If the ruler falls through completely, use the metre stick instead.

- Calculate the new average time.
- How much slower were you? Give the answer in seconds **and** as a percentage.
- Bonus: try with your non-writing hand, or with eyes closed and the dropper saying "now" (sound instead of sight).

## Think

Use the unit *The nervous system* to answer in your notebook:

1. List every part of your body the "ruler is falling!" message passed through, from eye to fingers.
2. Why did counting backwards slow you down? Which part of the chain was busy?
3. A nerve signal travels about 1–2 m through your body at around 50–100 m/s. Estimate that travel time. Is it most of your reaction time, or a small part? So where does the rest of the time go?
4. A 30 cm ruler can only measure reaction times up to about 0.25 s. Show why.

Keep your reaction time — it is one of your sign-off items.
