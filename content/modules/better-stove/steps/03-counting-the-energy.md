## The question

In step 1 you guessed what percentage of the wood's energy ended up in the water. Today you measure it for the three-stone fire. This number is your **baseline**: the thing your own stove has to beat.

## Theory: energy in, energy out (20 minutes)

Read **Energy and power**, and look back at the energy table in **Combustion and fuels**. You need three formulas:

- Energy into the water: $Q = mc\Delta T$, with $c = 4200$ J/kg°C for water.
- Energy in the fuel burnt: $E_{\text{fuel}} = m_{\text{wood}} \times 16{,}000{,}000$ J/kg.
- Efficiency $= \dfrac{Q}{E_{\text{fuel}}} \times 100\%$.

**Practice.** A pot of 1.0 kg of water warms from 18 °C to 92 °C on 0.25 kg of wood. Work out $Q$, $E_{\text{fuel}}$ and the efficiency before you go outside. Check with your guide.

Water in Addis Ababa boils at about 92 °C, not 100 °C, because the air pressure is lower at 2,400 m. Your thermometer will tell you your own boiling point: it is the temperature where the water stops getting hotter however hard it bubbles.

## Do: the Water Boiling Test (45 minutes)

This is a simple version of the test that stove engineers around the world use. The whole class runs one three-stone fire together, each team with a job. Swap jobs if you run it twice.

**Before lighting**

1. **Fuel team.** Weigh a bundle of dry sticks of about 500 g, plus a small handful of kindling. Record the total mass, $m_{\text{start}}$.
2. **Water team.** Put exactly 1.000 kg of water in the pot (weigh it, don't just measure the volume). Record its starting temperature.
3. **Pot team.** Set the pot firmly on the three stones. The stones should be the same ones, the same distance apart, every time you test.

**During the test**

4. Your guide lights the fire. Start the stopwatch the moment the kindling catches.
5. **Temperature team.** Read the temperature every 2 minutes, with the probe in the middle of the water, not touching the pot. Wear gloves and reach in from the upwind side.
6. Only the guide or the fuel team (with gloves, while the guide is at the fire) feeds sticks, only from the weighed bundle, keeping a steady fire.
7. **Smoke team.** Clip a white cloth square to a long stick and hold it in the smoke 50 cm above the pot for exactly 60 seconds, at 6 minutes after lighting. Stand upwind, arm out straight. Keep the cloth: it's your soot card.

**At the boil**

8. When the temperature stops rising and the water is bubbling hard, stop the clock. Record the **time to boil**.
9. Your guide pulls the burning sticks out with tongs and smothers them in sand. When they are out and cool enough, the fuel team weighs all the unburnt wood left over, plus anything left in the bundle: $m_{\text{left}}$.
10. Your guide scoops the glowing charcoal from the fire into a tin with a lid to smother it. Weigh it later (subtract the mass of the empty tin) as $m_{\text{char}}$.

## Calculate (15 minutes)

1. Wood burnt: $m_{\text{wood}} = m_{\text{start}} - m_{\text{left}}$.
2. $Q = 1.0 \times 4200 \times \Delta T$.
3. $E_{\text{fuel}} = m_{\text{wood}} \times 16{,}000{,}000$.
4. Efficiency.
5. **Do it better:** some of the wood didn't really burn, it just turned to charcoal, which still holds about 29 MJ/kg. Subtract $m_{\text{char}} \times 29{,}000{,}000$ J from $E_{\text{fuel}}$ and work out the efficiency again. Which number is fairer to the fire?

## Record

Your full data table, your temperature–time graph, every calculation with units, and your soot card (label it). Was your guess from step 1 too high or too low? Write down where you think the rest of the energy went.
