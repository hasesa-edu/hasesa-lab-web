# Better Stove: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 10 hours, best run as six sessions of 1.5–2 hours (step 4 needs a full 2-hour session), for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. Steps 1, 3, 5, 6 and 7 need an outdoor fire area and a dry day with little wind; on a wet day swap in step 2 (candles can be done under a covered, open-sided shelter) or the calculations.

You light every fire and you are responsible for every fire. Students feed sticks only when you are standing at that fire.

---

## Before the module

### 1. Collect and prepare the tins (two weeks ahead)

- **Oil tins, 4–5 L**, one per team plus two spares (one for your demonstration stove). Cooking-oil tins are best: thin, clean steel with no paint inside. Paint tins work only if they are completely empty.
- **Food cans, 7–10 cm across**, three per team plus spares, all the same diameter. Steel only (a magnet sticks). Aluminium drink cans melt. Avoid shiny, spangled galvanised tins.
- **Burn out every tin and can once** before students handle them: outdoors, on bare ground, with a small fire of paper and sticks inside each, everyone upwind. This burns off plastic linings, labels, glue and paint, which otherwise smoke badly and would spoil the soot comparison. Let them cool, then wash.

### 2. Build your demonstration stove (a week ahead)

Build one stove exactly as in step 4, so you know where the difficulties are and so you have a working stove for the step 1 race. Test it twice with 1 kg of water. Note your own times and wood use: they are your best guide to what students should get with your wood on your site.

### 3. Shopping and collecting

| Item | Where |
|---|---|
| Oil tins, food cans | Restaurants, cafés, neighbours; Minalesh Tera (the recycling market in Merkato) |
| Tin snips, files, pliers, hammer, nails | Hardware shops in Merkato and Piassa |
| Leather or welding gloves, safety glasses | Hardware shops, welding-supply shops |
| Bolts, nuts, plain (not galvanised) steel wire | Hardware shops |
| Two identical 2 L aluminium pots (*dist*) | Household goods shops |
| Digital probe thermometers (to 110 °C or more) | Kitchen-equipment shops; some pharmacies with lab supplies |
| Kitchen scale (1 g or 5 g steps, 2 kg or more) | Household goods shops |
| Eucalyptus firewood, one or two bundles | Local market and roadside sellers |
| Wood ash | Any household or injera bakery that cooks with wood or dung |
| Sand, clay soil | Construction sites, hardware yards, the ground |
| Candles, jars, white cloth, sticks, clothes pegs | Kiosks, household shops, old sheets |

**Firewood matters more than anything else for good results.** Buy all the wood for the module from one or two bundles a week early. Split it to thumb thickness (1.5–2 cm) and 25–30 cm long, and dry it in the sun every day. Damp wood smokes, stalls rocket stoves and ruins comparisons. Keep it covered and dry.

**Ash.** Sift it through an old sieve and make sure it is cold and completely dry. About 3 L per stove.

### 4. The fire area

Bare, level ground at least 5 m from buildings, dry grass, vehicles and stored fuel, sheltered from strong wind if possible. Mark a fire spot for each team about 3 m apart. A bucket of water and a bucket of dry sand at every fire spot, before anything is lit. A first-aid kit with burn dressings; know how to cool a burn (cool running water for 20 minutes).

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1; repeat the short version at every fire session)

- Fires only outdoors. **Never** a stove, charcoal or embers inside a room, not even "just to finish the coffee". Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills people in Addis every year.
- Only you light fires. Students feed only when you are at that fire, with gloves, one hand, face turned aside, from the upwind side.
- Water and sand buckets in place before lighting. Hair tied back; netela, scarves and loose sleeves off or tucked in.
- Everything near a fire is hot until you say it isn't. Gloves or tongs for every pot, can and stove.
- Nobody breathes, sniffs or waves smoke. Soot cards are held on 1.5 m sticks, arm straight, from upwind.
- Cutting tins: gloves and glasses on; every cut edge filed or folded before it leaves the table.
- At the end: you pour water on every fire, stir and pour again until the ashes are cold to the back of your hand held just above them.

## Session plans

### Step 1: Three stones and a tin (45 min)

Set up both fires before students arrive (unlit), with weighed 500 g bundles. Put exactly 1 kg of water in each pot. Light both together. Let students run the thermometers and stopwatch; you feed both fires steadily, trying to be fair to the three-stone fire (keep the sticks pushed in and the fire bright).

Hold the soot cloths yourself at about 6 minutes. Don't explain anything yet. Collect the efficiency guesses: most students guess far too high (50–80%).

**Typical result with dry eucalyptus, 1 kg of water starting around 17 °C:**

| | Three-stone fire | Tin-can stove |
|---|---|---|
| Time to boil | 15–25 min | 9–15 min |
| Wood burnt | 180–300 g | 80–150 g |
| Soot cloth | grey to black | faint grey |

If your stove doesn't clearly win in your practice runs, find out why before the module (usually damp wood, too little gap under the pot, or ash fallen into the tube).

### Step 2: What a fire needs (80 min)

**Candles and jars.** Use jars from about 0.3 L to 2 L. Times vary with candle and jar shape, but expect roughly 6–12 s for a 0.5 L jar, 15–25 s for 1 L and 30–50 s for 2 L, so the graph is close to a straight line through the origin: time is roughly proportional to the volume of air, so the candle is using something up from the air. Let the jar cool between repeats, and wipe the condensation off, or the times creep down.

A good extra point for stronger students: the candle goes out long before *all* the oxygen is gone (it stops at around 15–16% oxygen instead of 21%), because the flame needs enough oxygen reaching it fast enough to stay hot.

**Soot spoon.** Hold the spoon with tongs; the handle gets hot. The yellow part leaves soot quickly; the blue base leaves little or none. Yellow = glowing unburnt carbon.

**Answers.**

1. Water droplets come from the hydrogen in the wax; soot from the carbon.
2. Fuel: pull sticks out of the fire. Oxygen: a lid over a pan, or smothering embers with sand or the ash. Heat: water on a fire; spreading the embers so they cool.
3. The three-stone fire was more like the yellow tip: oxygen doesn't mix well with the burning gases, and the cold air and pot cool the flame, so combustion is incomplete. The stove was closer to the blue base: more complete.
4. Look for: carbon monoxide has no smell; it stops blood carrying oxygen; people fall asleep and don't wake; always burn charcoal outside and never take a glowing stove inside, even after the flames have gone (glowing charcoal makes the most CO).

**Calculations.** 2 × 16 = 32 MJ. 2 × 29 = 58 MJ. 0.5 × 22 = 11 MJ, less than the 16 MJ in 1 kg of wood.

### Step 3: Counting the energy (80 min)

**Practice answer.** $\Delta T = 74$ °C, $Q = 1.0 \times 4200 \times 74 = 310{,}800$ J. $E_{\text{fuel}} = 0.25 \times 16{,}000{,}000 = 4{,}000{,}000$ J. Efficiency $\approx 7.8\%$.

Run one three-stone fire for the whole class; give every team a job and make sure each team copies the full data set. If time allows, run it twice with jobs swapped.

**Putting out and weighing.** At the boil, pull the burning sticks out with tongs onto sand and smother them by pressing them into the sand (don't use water: wet wood weighs more). Brush off the sand before weighing. Scoop the glowing charcoal into a tin with a lid (weigh the empty tin first); it goes out in a few minutes and can be weighed when cool.

**Expected numbers.** Boiling point in Addis about 92–93 °C (it varies a little with weather and with your thermometer). Wood burnt 180–300 g, charcoal left 15–40 g, efficiency 7–12% without the charcoal correction, 9–14% with it.

**Worked example** (a realistic run): water 1.000 kg, 17 °C → 92 °C, $Q = 4200 \times 75 = 315{,}000$ J. Wood burnt 240 g: $E = 0.24 \times 16{,}000{,}000 = 3{,}840{,}000$ J, efficiency $= 8.2\%$. Charcoal left 20 g holds $0.02 \times 29{,}000{,}000 = 580{,}000$ J, so the energy really used was $3{,}260{,}000$ J and the efficiency $9.7\%$.

**Where the rest went** (what students should list): heating the air that rushes past the pot, heat radiated sideways from the open fire, heating the stones and ground, smoke (unburnt fuel), charcoal left, and some water evaporating.

**Extension for strong students:** weigh the pot and water after boiling. Water that evaporated took about 2.26 MJ/kg with it. 30 g lost is $0.03 \times 2{,}260{,}000 \approx 68{,}000$ J, which is a fifth more useful energy on top of the 315,000 J. Real stove tests include it.

### Step 4: Build the stove (120 min)

Do the cutting at a table, not on knees. Supervise snips closely. For the side holes, the easiest method is: punch a row of nail holes round the drawn circle, then join them with snips. Students under about 15, or anyone struggling, can mark and file while you or a confident student cuts.

**Where students get stuck:**

- *The L joint.* The hole in the chimney must be slightly smaller than the feed tube so the tabs grip it. Too big and ash pours into the fire.
- *Chimney height.* It should be at least 2–3 times its diameter (a 7.5 cm can needs about 15–20 cm of chimney above the feed tube). A short chimney draws weakly; add a third can.
- *No gap under the pot.* A pot sitting directly on the chimney chokes the stove: smoke pours out of the feed tube. Aim for 1.5–2 cm.
- *Fuel shelf missing or too low.* Without air under the sticks, the stove smoulders.
- *Ash in the tube.* Pour slowly and cover the chimney mouth with a rag while filling.

Let the clay cap dry while you do the test fires (it will crack a little; that's fine).

**Durability.** Thin food cans inside the fire burn through after perhaps 10–20 fires. That's a useful discussion point for step 7 (real improved stoves use ceramic or thick steel liners). Keep spare cans.

### Step 5: The boiling race (100 min)

Run four or five student stoves and one three-stone fire at the same time. You won't be able to stand at every fire at once, so stagger lighting by a minute or two and stay at the fire being fed; teams start their clocks at their own lighting.

**Expected results** (one realistic team):

| | Three-stone fire | Tin-can stove |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 1.000 kg, 17 → 92 °C | 1.000 kg, 17 → 92 °C |
| Time to boil | 18 min | 11 min |
| Wood burnt | 240 g | 110 g |
| $Q$ | 315,000 J | 315,000 J |
| $E_{\text{fuel}}$ | 3,840,000 J | 1,760,000 J |
| Efficiency | 8.2% | 17.9% |
| Firepower $E/t$ | 3,840,000 ÷ 1080 ≈ 3.6 kW | 1,760,000 ÷ 660 ≈ 2.7 kW |

Wood saved $= (240 - 110) \div 240 \times 100\% \approx 54\%$. The stove is about $17.9 \div 8.2 \approx 2.2$ times as efficient. Student stoves typically reach 15–25% efficiency and save 30–60% of the wood; a first stove that only saves 20% is still a success, and a good topic for step 6.

Notice that the stove has *less* firepower but boils *faster*: much more of its energy reaches the pot. That is a good question to ask.

**Graph answers.** The stove's curve is steeper: the gradient is the rate of temperature rise, so more power is reaching the water. Both curves flatten at the boiling point because extra energy goes into turning water into steam, not raising the temperature.

**Soot cards.** Three-stone cloths score 3–5; stove cloths 0–2. If a stove's cloth is as dark as the three-stone one, it's usually damp wood or overfeeding.

### Step 6: Make it better (90 min)

The pot skirt test is the quickest and most reliable; steer most teams towards it. The insulation test needs two teams with identical stoves, one filled with sand, and works best from a cold start.

**Expected results:**

- **Pot skirt** (1 cm gap): typically 10–20% less time and wood. With only two runs each, the difference is sometimes within the ranges, which is a good discussion about how many repeats you need.
- **Gap under the pot:** 1.5–2 cm usually beats 3 cm (hot gases are kept closer to the pot). Below about 1 cm the stove chokes and smokes, so there is a best gap, not "smaller is always better".
- **Insulation:** ash beats sand, most clearly from a cold start. Sand is denser and conducts heat better, so it soaks up more of the fire's heat before the pot gets it, and keeps the tube cooler (more smoke).

**Common faults:** starting run B with water already warm from run A; using a different pot; changing two things at once (for example a new pot support *and* the skirt); not weighing every bundle.

### Step 7: Smoke, health and your verdict (85 min)

Keep the final demonstration moving; do sign-off conversations while other teams run their stoves.

**Scale-up answers** (with 54% saved): $6 \times 0.54 \approx 3.2$ kg a day; $3.24 \times 365 \approx 1180$ kg a year; $1183 \times 16 \approx 18{,}900$ MJ a year. Students use their own percentage, so answers vary.

**Discussion points:**

- In a kitchen without a chimney, the smoke stays in the room and is breathed in, mostly by whoever cooks (usually women and girls) and the small children near them. Babies on their mother's back breathe it all day.
- Even a better stove makes CO, so it must be used outdoors or in a very well ventilated kitchen, and never left burning in a room where people sleep.
- Chimneys take smoke out but cost money and must be kept clean. Biogas burns almost clean but needs animals and a digester. Cooking outside works in the dry season but not in the rains.
- Real limits: the Mirt stove exists because injera baking needs a large, even heat that a small rocket stove can't provide; a stove that saves wood but is slow or awkward may not be used.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Smoke pours back out of the feed tube | Pot too close to the chimney; feed tube overfilled; no air space under the fuel shelf; chimney too short. |
| Stove smoulders and won't roar | Damp wood; sticks too thick; ash has fallen into the tube; fuel shelf missing. |
| Outside of the tin very hot all over | Insulation has gaps or settled: tap and top up the ash. |
| Water temperature stops at 88–90 °C | Probe touching steam or the pot side; or the true boiling point on that day. Check with a second thermometer. |
| Wood left weighs more than at the start | Sand or water on the sticks. Brush off sand; never quench with water before weighing. |
| Inner can burns through | Normal after many fires. Replace the L from spare cans. |

## Judging the sign-off items

- **stove-works:** watch it boil 1 kg of water on sticks only (no paper stuffing once lit). In the step 5 table, wood burnt must be less than the three-stone fire's on the same day. It doesn't have to be a big saving.
- **explains-combustion:** must link a side of the fire triangle (oxygen reaching the fire, heat kept in by insulation) to *more complete* combustion and less smoke, and say why CO is deadly (no smell; stops blood carrying oxygen) and that charcoal never burns indoors. "Because it's a better stove" isn't enough.
- **boiling-test:** check the table has masses before and after, start and boiling temperatures, and times for both fires, and the graph has both curves on one set of axes with labelled axes, units and a key.
- **efficiency:** check $\Delta T$ uses their measured boiling point (not 100 °C), masses are in kg, and units appear on every line. Efficiencies above about 40% for their stove mean an error, usually grams used as kilograms or the wood left not subtracted.
- **fair-test:** one variable changed, at least five controls listed *and* kept (fresh cold water every run, same pot, same wood), at least two runs per setting, means and ranges. A conclusion that says "no clear difference" is fine if the data supports it.
- **smoke-and-recommendation:** the recommendation uses their own numbers (time, wood, efficiency, soot score), gives a clear piece of advice, states the CO rule, and names a real limit of the test.
- **safety:** if a student broke a fire or tool rule after a reminder, talk with them before signing.
