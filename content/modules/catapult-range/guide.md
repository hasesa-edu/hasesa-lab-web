# Catapult Range: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 8 hours, best run as five sessions of 1.5–2 hours, for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. Catapults throw 1–6 m, so you don't need a field: a school hall, a long corridor or a courtyard at least 8 m long works, and indoors is better because wind blows paper balls and ping-pong balls about.

| Session | Steps | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 The mystery shot, start of 2 (design and sketch) | 45 + 30 min |
| 2 | 2 Build your catapult (build, scale, first shots) | 60 min, plus 30 min of step 3 (plan, stretch) |
| 3 | 3 Stretch and range (fire, graph) | 45 min, plus 45 min of step 4 |
| 4 | 4 The angle question (finish), 5 How fast? | 30 + 75 min |
| 5 | 6 Predict the landing, 7 Range day | 60 + 60 min |

Total: 480 minutes.

---

## Before the module

### 1. Build your own catapult (a week ahead)

You need one working catapult for the hook, with a calibration graph already drawn, so the "mystery shot" lands first time. Build it exactly as the students will (step 2) so you meet their problems first:

- **Base:** two slats 40 cm long, two crossbars 20 cm, nailed or screwed.
- **Pivot:** two small blocks (about 5 × 5 cm) fixed near the front of the base, 2–3 cm apart. Drill a hole through both blocks and through the bottom of the arm, and push a long nail or a bolt through. The hole in the arm should be slightly bigger than the nail so it swings freely; the blocks should be tight so it doesn't wobble.
- **Arm:** a slat about 35 cm long, 2–3 cm wide.
- **Uprights and stop bar:** two sticks about 20 cm tall, fixed about 10 cm behind the pivot, one each side of the arm, with a crossbar on top. Adjust the height or position until the arm stops at about 60° to the ground. That gives a launch angle of about 30° on flat ground.
- **Rubber:** two inner-tube strips, about 1.5 cm wide, each tied in a loop from the top of an upright to the middle of the arm. Two strips are enough for 4–5 m with a 20 g bean bag.
- **Cup:** a metal tablespoon, taped tightly with electrical tape. A bottle cap glued or nailed on also works.
- **Pull-back scale:** cardboard beside the arm, marks every 10° of pull-back from the stop (0) to flat (about 60).

Fire three shots at each of five settings and draw your calibration graph. Re-check it on the morning of session 1; rubber weakens with age and heat.

### 2. Shopping and gathering

- **Wood:** carpentry workshops cut offcuts all day. Ask for straight pieces about 2 × 4 cm in cross-section; most will give a sackful for very little. Pre-cut a kit for each team (two 40 cm, two 20 cm, two 20 cm uprights, one 20 cm stop bar, one 35 cm arm, two pivot blocks) so build time goes into building, not sawing.
- **Inner tubes:** bicycle repair shops throw away punctured tubes. Ask for several; cut them into 1.5 cm strips with strong scissors. Wash them first: they are dusty and greasy.
- **Rubber bands:** a bag of thick ones from a stationery shop, as a backup and for cardboard-box catapults.
- **Nails, screws, a hammer, a hand drill, a small saw, electrical tape, duct tape, string:** hardware shops.
- **Bean bags:** about 20 g of dried ater (field peas) or small beans in a cloth square, tied with string or a rubber band. Students make their own in step 2, but make 10 spares, all weighed to within 1 g. A tailor will give you a bag of cloth scraps.
- **Safety glasses:** one pair per student, from a hardware or workwear shop. Non-negotiable.
- **Targets:** two or three plastic basins about 40 cm across, from any market.
- **Tape measures:** at least one 10 m tape per two teams, plus metre sticks.
- **Phones:** check before session 4 that each team has access to a phone that records video and can be scrubbed frame by frame. Most Android and iPhone gallery apps can. If not, pair teams up or use yours.

### 3. Prepare the range

Mark a **firing line** across the room with chalk or tape. Catapults sit on the line; everyone stands behind it. Lay tape measures along the floor in front of each firing position, starting at the line. Leave at least 2 m between firing lanes. Nobody, ever, stands at the target end while catapults are loaded.

Teach and use two calls: **"Range hot"** (catapults may be loaded and fired, everyone behind the line) and **"Range clear"** (all arms resting at 0, nothing in any cup, students may walk forward to measure and collect). Only you call them.

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1, repeat at the start of every firing session)

- Soft projectiles only. Confiscate any stone, bottle cap, marble or coin found near a catapult. Bottle caps are used **as the cup**, never as ammunition.
- Never aim at a person or animal. A student who does, even unloaded, sits out the rest of the session. Say this on day one and do it.
- Glasses on before "range hot". Inner-tube strips can snap and whip back at face height.
- Load and fire from the side of the catapult, not from behind the arm. Fingers away from the stop bar: the arm hits it hard.
- Check rubber before each session: discard cracked, sticky, sun-damaged or nicked strips. Don't stretch strips more than about three times their length.
- You do the drilling, sawing and hammering, or hold the work while one student uses the tool.
- Catapults go home at the end. Repeat the rules then, because students will be tempted to fire harder things at siblings.

## Session plans

### Step 1: The mystery shot (45 min)

Place a basin at a distance you have tested (for example 3.5 m), measure it in front of the class, read the setting from your graph, and fire. Practise beforehand so it lands. Then fire the short, long and steep shots without explaining. Don't answer their questions yet; collect the guesses for the best angle on the board and keep them.

**Expected misconceptions:** many students think the bag keeps being pushed forward after it leaves the cup, that it stops dead at the top of its path, and that 90° (straight up) or 60° throws furthest.

### Step 2: Build your catapult (90 min)

Hand out pre-cut kits. Teams sketch first. Most time goes into the pivot and the frame. Walk round with the drill.

The most common faults:

- **Wobbly pivot:** the arm twists sideways, so shots go left and right. Tighten the blocks, add a washer.
- **Base slides when fired:** put a book on the back of the base or tape it to the floor. Sliding is the biggest cause of poor repeatability.
- **Cup twists:** re-tape more tightly, or put a small nail through the spoon handle.
- **Rubber slips on the arm:** cut a small notch in the arm for the loop to sit in.

**Expected first results:** three shots at setting 40 with two inner-tube strips and a 20 g bag land about 2.5–3.5 m away, within 20–40 cm of each other once the base is held still.

### Step 3: Stretch and range (75 min)

Controlled variables to look for: same bean bag (or same mass), same rubber strips, same tilt (flat), same position on the firing line, same person firing, same way of releasing the arm (let go cleanly, don't push).

**Expected results** (2 inner-tube strips, 35 cm arm, 20 g bag, flat floor, launch about 30°):

| Setting | Stretch (cm) | Mean range (m) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | about 4 | 1.2 |
| 30 | about 7 | 2.0 |
| 40 | about 10 | 3.0 |
| 50 | about 12 | 3.9 |
| 60 | about 14 | 4.6 |

Your numbers will differ; what matters is the shape. Range rises steadily with stretch, close to a straight line in the middle, often flattening at the largest settings. Doubling the stretch usually gives a bit more than double the range at small settings and less at large ones. Why it is not simple: rubber doesn't stretch like a perfect spring, a lot of energy goes into moving the arm itself, and more is lost in the bang against the stop bar (the louder the bang, the more energy wasted). Students who notice "it doesn't double" have done good science; don't force a straight-line answer.

Spread (biggest minus smallest) of 20–40 cm is normal. More than 60 cm means a build problem: fix it before step 4.

### Step 4: The angle question (75 min)

The geometry: the bag leaves at right angles to the arm, so launch angle ≈ 90° − (arm angle at the stop). With the stop at 60°, that is 30° on flat ground. Tilting the base forward (blocks under the front) adds the tilt angle. Wedges of 10°, 20°, 30° and 40° cut from offcuts, labelled, save a lot of time. To get 20°, put a 10° wedge under the back.

The video check: pausing just after release and measuring the path angle on screen typically agrees with 90° − a to within 5°. The bag actually leaves a little before the arm stops, so the real angle is often a few degrees higher than calculated.

**Expected results** (setting 40):

| Launch angle | Mean range (m) |
|---|---|
| 20° | 2.4 |
| 30° | 3.0 |
| 40° | 3.3 |
| 50° | 3.1 |
| 60° | 2.6 |
| 70° | 1.8 |

The peak is usually between 35° and 45°, and it is broad: 40° and 50° may be within the spread of each other. Don't let teams claim a precise best angle; "around 40°" is the right level of claim. Pairs like 30° and 60° give similar ranges, but the higher one is usually a little shorter because the release point is higher when tilted forward and air drag acts for longer. Paper balls and ping-pong balls go much less far (often half to two-thirds of the bean bag's range) and their best angle is lower, because air resistance matters more for light objects.

**Where students get stuck:** they move the stop bar instead of tilting the base. Ask: "When you moved the stop bar, what else changed?" (The stretch at release.) Also, some tilt the base sideways. Use a protractor along the long side of the base.

### Step 5: How fast? (75 min)

The two-coin demo: flick sharply with a ruler pivoting on one finger. They should hear one click.

**Expected results** (setting 40, flat, about 30° launch, range about 3.0 m): flight time about 0.6–0.7 s, which is 18–20 frames at 30 frames per second. Horizontal speed about 3.0 ÷ 0.62 ≈ 4.8 m/s. Upward speed about 9.8 × 0.31 ≈ 3.0 m/s. tan θ ≈ 0.63, so θ ≈ 32°, close to the 30° from geometry. Launch speed √(4.8² + 3.0²) ≈ 5.7 m/s.

The bag is released about 25–30 cm above the floor, so the trip down is a little longer than the trip up. This makes the calculated upward speed slightly too big. Strong students can spot this from the video (the top is a bit before halfway).

**Where students get stuck:** counting frames. Get them to find the first frame where the bag is clear of the cup and the first frame where it touches the floor, then subtract the frame numbers (or the time stamps, if the app shows them). In slow-motion mode, they must divide by the recording rate (120 or 240), not by 30. A flight time of several seconds means they forgot this.

Stopwatch teams typically get times 0.1–0.3 s too long (reaction time at the end is worse than at the start, because the landing is harder to anticipate). That is a good discussion point.

### Step 6: Predict the landing (60 min)

Call distances that are inside each team's tested range. Include one between two settings so they must interpolate. Good teams land within about 30 cm. If a team's misses are all on one side, their rubber has changed since step 3: get them to re-fire three settings and redraw.

Speed × time check: at setting 40, 4.8 m/s × 0.62 s ≈ 3.0 m. It agrees by definition if they use the same shots; it is more interesting with a different setting, where they predict using a measured speed and time for that setting.

### Step 7: Range day (60 min)

Place a different basin distance for each team, between 1.5 m and 4.5 m, and not exactly on one of their tested settings. Do the sign-off conversations while other teams shoot. A reasonable success rate is one hit in three shots for most teams; a team that hits on the first shot from a written prediction has earned real applause.

## Answer key for the concept unit (Projectile motion)

- horizontal-speed: 4.0 ÷ 0.80 = 5.0 m/s.
- vertical-speed: 9.8 × 0.50 = 4.9 m/s.
- drop-vs-push: they land at the same time.
- table-launch: t = √(2 × 0.80 ÷ 9.8) ≈ 0.40 s; 3.0 × 0.40 ≈ 1.21 m.
- best-angle: 45° (lower in practice for light balls).
- partner-angles: about the same distance.
- double-speed: 4 times as far.
- launch-angle-tan: tan⁻¹(4.4 ÷ 5.6) ≈ 38°.

## Questions to ask

- "Once the bag leaves the spoon, what is pushing it forward?" (Nothing. It keeps going because nothing stops it.)
- "At the top of the flight, is it moving?" (Yes, sideways.)
- "Your graph flattens at high settings. Where is the extra energy going?"
- "Why does a very high angle throw short, when it stays up longest?"
- "If your three shots spread over 40 cm, can you ever be sure of hitting a 40 cm basin?"
- "What would happen to your graph if you used a 40 g bag?" (Shorter ranges at every setting; worth testing as an extension.)

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Shots go left and right | Pivot wobbles, cup twisted, or rubber strips unequal. Tighten, re-tape, match the strips. |
| Range keeps falling during a session | Rubber tiring or slipping. Retie, or replace strips and re-calibrate. |
| Bag hits the stop bar or goes straight up | Stop bar too far back (arm stops too early), or the cup is too deep. Move the stop bar, use a flatter spoon. |
| Bag dribbles out before launch | Released too slowly or cup too shallow. Let go cleanly; use a deeper spoon or bottle cap. |
| Base jumps when fired | Too light. Add a brick or bag of sand on the back, or tape it down. |
| Rubber snaps | Old or nicked tube, or over-stretched. Replace, and check that glasses were on. |

## Judging the sign-off items

- **builds:** fire three shots at one setting in front of you. All within about 50 cm of each other. A catapult that needed fixing along the way is fine.
- **stretch-graph:** at least four settings, three shots each, mean and spread calculated correctly, axes labelled with units, a line or curve of best fit (not dot-to-dot).
- **angle-test:** check the plan lists pull-back, bean bag and rubber as controlled, and that they really changed the angle by tilting (not by moving the stop bar). The conclusion should be a range of angles ("about 35–45°"), not a single exact number.
- **launch-speed:** working shows frames (or stopwatch times), frames per second, flight time in seconds, range in metres, and horizontal speed in m/s. A plausible value is 3–8 m/s.
- **hits-target:** the predicted setting must be on the class board before the first shot. One of three in or touching the basin. If they miss all three but their misses are close (within about 30 cm) and they can explain why from their spread, you may let them try one more round after re-calibrating.
- **explains:** must mention the energy chain (elastic store to kinetic), that more stretch stores more energy so the bag leaves faster, and the trade-off of angle (low angle: little time in the air; high angle: little sideways speed).
- **safety:** if a student broke a rule after a reminder, discuss it with them before signing.
