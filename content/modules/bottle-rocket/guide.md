# Bottle Rocket: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is about 8 hours, best run as five sessions of about 1.5 hours, for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. Steps 4–7 need an open field and a dry, calm day; plan an indoor fallback (step 3 or the theory reading) for rainy days.

---

## Before the module

### 1. Build the launcher (a week ahead)

Students build rockets, not launchers. One good launcher serves the whole class. The design below is the well-known **cable-tie launcher**: the bottle's neck ring is held by a crown of cable ties, and pulling a sleeve down releases them all at once. Releasing at a known pressure is what makes fair tests possible.

| Part | Notes |
|---|---|
| ½-inch (21 mm outside) PVC pipe, about 50 cm, with an end cap | Plumbing shops. 21 mm pipe fits snugly inside a standard fizzy-drink bottle neck. |
| A bicycle inner-tube valve (Schrader) | Cut from an old inner tube at a bicycle repair shop, with a ring of rubber left around it. |
| Strong epoxy glue | To seal the valve through the end cap. |
| A short piece of 32 mm PVC pipe (about 5 cm) and a hose clamp | Holds the cable ties around the launch tube. |
| 10–12 cable ties, 20 cm long | |
| A piece of 40 mm PVC pipe (about 6 cm) | The release sleeve. It slides over the cable ties. |
| 2–3 m of strong string | The release cord. |
| A board about 40 × 40 cm, and pipe clips or a wooden frame | To hold the launch tube vertical. Tent pegs or stakes to fix the board. |
| Electrical tape or a rubber O-ring | Wrap tape around the top of the launch tube until the bottle slides on with a firm, airtight fit. |

**Assembly in brief.** Drill the end cap, push the valve through and epoxy it airtight. Glue the cap onto the bottom of the 21 mm tube. Mount the tube vertically on the board. Slide the 32 mm collar onto the tube about 3 cm below the top and clamp the cable ties around it, pointing up, so their heads sit just above the bottle's neck ring when a bottle is pushed on. Put the 40 mm sleeve over the ties: pushed up, it squeezes the heads over the neck ring and locks the bottle; pulled down (by the string), the ties spring open and the rocket goes.

**Test it** with an old bottle full of water to 4 bar, from behind the line. It must hold for a minute without leaking, then release cleanly.

### 2. Shopping

- Ask cafés for empty fizzy-drink bottles: at least 3 per team plus 10 spares. Reject any dented or deeply scratched ones.
- A floor pump with a gauge. Check the gauge against a second gauge if you can (bicycle shops have one).
- Modelling clay is sold in stationery shops. Coins taped in the nose cone work too.

### 3. Choose the field

At least 50 × 50 m, flat, grassy if possible, with no road, building or power line within reach. Mark a **line** 10 m from the launcher with rope or chalk. Check the weather: postpone if the wind is strong enough to move small branches.

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1, repeat at every launch session)

- Glasses on, everyone behind the line. You do the countdown, you pull the cord. Nobody else.
- Before pumping, look at the bottle: no dents, cracks or deep scratches; count its launches on the label and retire it after ten.
- **Never above 4 bar (60 psi).** Most launches use 3 bar.
- A rocket that doesn't release: you let the air out through the pump connector (unscrew it slowly) before anyone approaches. Never lean over a pressurised bottle.
- Students do not catch rockets. Watch it land; you say when to collect.

## Session plans

### Step 1: The bottle that flies (45 min)

Read the rules, then launch three times: a third full (the "good" launch), empty, and nearly full. All at 3 bar. Students must notice that **empty** and **full** both fly badly. Don't explain yet. Collect the "height guesses" for step 5.

### Step 2: Push and push back (75 min)

Balloon rockets work best with long sausage balloons and a smooth string (fishing line is ideal). Steer the discussion to: **the balloon pushes the air out, the air pushes the balloon**. A common wrong idea is that "the air pushes on the air behind it". Counter-example: rockets work in space.

Force diagrams: thrust much longer than weight at launch; at burnout, only weight (and air resistance) remain, both pointing down while it's still rising.

### Step 3: Squeezed air (60 min)

Pump an empty bottle on the launcher with the sleeve **locked**, then release the pressure through the valve, so nothing launches. The gauge rises roughly evenly per stroke at first.

**Expected answers.** Inside mouth diameter about 2.2 cm, so r = 1.1 cm, A ≈ 3.8 cm² = 0.00038 m². At 300 kPa, F ≈ 114 N. A rocket of 0.12 kg with 0.6 kg of water has a weight of about 7 N, so the push is roughly 15 times the weight.

### Step 4: Build your rocket (90 min)

The most common faults: fins too small, fins twisted, no nose weight. Rockets without nose weight tumble. Encourage the swing test; it really works. Check each bottle before the first launch.

### Step 5: How high? (75 min)

Practise the clinometer indoors first. Students often read the angle from the wrong side; the level-straw check (90° on the scale means 0° of tilt) fixes this. Expect a good 2 L rocket at 3 bar to reach roughly 15–30 m. Heights much bigger than 40 m are probably reading errors near 90°: move the observers back.

### Step 6: The water question (90 min)

Make sure teams keep pressure, rocket and nose cone the same. **Expected result:** the best amount is usually around one-third of the bottle (about 600 mL in a 2 L bottle), with a broad peak. The empty bottle flies a few metres; a nearly full one only a little more.

Why there is a best amount: with little water there is little mass to throw out, so the total push is small. With lots of water, there's not much squeezed air, so its pressure drops quickly as it expands and the push dies before the water is gone; meanwhile the rocket is heavy.

Teams will probably finish only 12 launches in the session. That's fine: they can share data across teams if everyone used the same plan.

### Step 7: Launch day (60 min)

Keep launches moving: one team pumps while the next prepares. Do the sign-off conversations while others launch.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Water leaks while pumping | Add a turn of tape to the launch tube, or replace the O-ring. |
| Rocket releases early | Cable-tie heads not over the neck ring: push the bottle further down before locking the sleeve. |
| Rocket tumbles | Add nose weight (10 g at a time) or bigger fins; check fins are straight. |
| Rocket curves sideways | Fins unequal or twisted; launcher not vertical; wind. |
| Gauge doesn't move | The pump connector isn't fully on the valve, or the valve is blocked. |

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **flies:** two straight flights, not necessarily high. A rocket that tumbles once and is fixed is fine.
- **explains-launch:** must mention the water being pushed down and the rocket being pushed up, as a pair of forces. "The air pushes the ground" isn't enough.
- **measures-height:** the working shows d, θ, tan θ and eye height. The answer should be plausible (roughly 5–40 m).
- **fair-test:** check the controlled variables are listed **and** were really kept the same (same pressure every time).
- **graph:** correct axes and units, a curve of best fit (not dot-to-dot), and a reason based on the push from the air and the mass of the water.
- **safety:** if a student broke a rule after a reminder, discuss it with them before signing.
