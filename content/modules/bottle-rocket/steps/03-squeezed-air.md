## Do: feel the squeeze (20 minutes)

Take a needle-free syringe. Pull the plunger out halfway. Put your finger firmly over the end.

1. Push the plunger in. How hard is it? Let go: what happens?
2. Pull it out. What do you feel now? Let go.
3. Fill the syringe halfway with **water** instead, finger on the end, and try to push. What's different?

Air can be squeezed into a smaller space, and it pushes back. Water hardly squeezes at all.

## Do: pump a bottle (15 minutes, with the guide)

Your guide fixes an **empty** bottle on the launcher, without letting it launch, and pumps slowly while you watch from behind the line. Call out the gauge reading every five strokes. Is the pressure going up by the same amount each time?

Then the guide lets the air out through the valve. Listen to it.

## Notice something puzzling

The bottle's mouth is only about the size of a coin, and the pressure on the gauge is a few "bar". How can that be enough to throw a rocket higher than a building?

## Theory: pressure

Read **Pressure**. You'll learn:

- that pressure is force spread over an area, $P = F/A$,
- why pumping more air into a bottle raises the pressure,
- what the units on the gauge mean (psi, bar, kPa),
- why Addis Ababa has lower air pressure than the coast,
- why only fizzy-drink bottles are allowed.

## Do it better: the force on the nozzle (15 minutes)

1. Measure the inside diameter of a bottle's mouth with a ruler, in centimetres.
2. Work out its area: $A = \pi r^2$. Convert to m² (divide cm² by 10,000).
3. For a launch pressure of 3 bar (300 kPa), calculate the force on the water in the nozzle, $F = P \times A$.
4. Weigh a full rocket on the kitchen scale and work out its weight in newtons ($W = mg$).

How many times bigger is the push than the weight?

## Record

Write the force calculation in your notebook, with units on every line. Add one sentence: what is the squeezed air doing to the water, and what is the water doing to the rocket?
