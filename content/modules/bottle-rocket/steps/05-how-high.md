## Do: guess, then measure (10 minutes)

Pick something tall near the school: a tree, a flagpole, the corner of a building. Everyone writes down a guess of its height.

How could you check without climbing it?

## Theory: measuring heights with angles

Read **Measuring heights with angles**. You'll learn:

- how your eye, the ground and the top of a tall thing make a right-angled triangle,
- the tangent ratio, $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$, and how to rearrange it to $h = d\tan\theta$,
- how to make and read a clinometer,
- why you should stand far enough back that the angle is between about 30° and 60°.

## Build: a clinometer (15 minutes)

1. Tape a straw along the straight edge of a protractor.
2. Tie a thread through the hole in the middle and hang a washer on it.
3. Hold the straw level: the thread should cross 90°. That is your zero.
4. Practise: look through the straw at the top of a door, pinch the thread, and read the angle.

## Do: measure the tree (25 minutes)

1. Measure 15–20 m from the base of the tree with the tape.
2. Three people each measure the angle to the top. Take the mean.
3. Measure each observer's eye height.
4. Calculate $h = d\tan\theta + \text{eye height}$.

How close were the guesses? Whose method was best?

## Do it better: measure a launch (25 minutes)

Rockets move fast, so plan:

- Put **two observers** at different distances from the launcher (for example 20 m and 30 m), on the same side.
- Each looks through their clinometer and follows the rocket up, then pinches the thread at its highest point.
- Calculate two heights. If they're close, take the mean. If not, the rocket probably drifted sideways: launch again.

Measure two launches this way.

## Record

For each measurement write $d$, $\theta$, eye height and $h$, with the working. Compare to your guess from step 1.
