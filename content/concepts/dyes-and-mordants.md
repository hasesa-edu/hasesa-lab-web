Every colour you see on a netela border, a kemis or a scarf is there because some molecule is **taking light away**. This unit explains how, how dye is made to stay on cloth, and how to measure whether it stays.

## Why a dye has colour

White light, like sunlight, is a mixture of all the colours of the rainbow. When light hits a dye, the dye **absorbs** some colours and lets the rest bounce off or pass through. Your eye sees only what is left.

| The dye absorbs mostly… | …so it looks |
|---|---|
| violet and blue | yellow |
| blue and blue-green | orange |
| green | red to purple (magenta) |
| yellow and orange | blue |
| red | blue-green |

Pairs of colours across this table are **complementary**: take one away from white light and you see the other.

Which colours a molecule absorbs depends on its **shape**. Dye molecules have a long chain or ring of atoms joined by alternating single and double bonds. Electrons spread out along this chain, and a longer chain absorbs longer-wavelength light. The part of the molecule that causes the colour is called the **chromophore** ("colour carrier").

The main dyes in this module:

| Plant | Amharic | Main colouring molecules | Colour on cotton |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onion skins | ሽንኩርት ልጣጭ (shinkurt lit'ach) | flavonoids (quercetin) | gold, orange-brown |
| Turmeric | እርድ (ird) | curcumin | bright yellow |
| Hibiscus (karkade) | ካርካዴ | anthocyanins | pink to mauve-grey |
| Red cabbage | ቀይ ጎመን (k'ey gomen) | anthocyanins | lilac, blue-grey |
| Coffee grounds, tea | ቡና, ሻይ | tannins and browned compounds | beige to brown |
| Eucalyptus leaves | ባሕር ዛፍ (bahir zaf) | tannins and flavonoids | yellow-brown, rust |

## Colour and pH

If a dye molecule gains or loses a hydrogen ion (H⁺), its shape changes, and so does the light it absorbs. That is why many plant dyes are also **pH indicators**. Anthocyanins (karkade, red cabbage) go red in acid and blue-green in alkali. Curcumin (turmeric) is yellow in acid and neutral, and red-brown in strong alkali. See **Acids, bases and pH**.

This matters to a dyer: the **same dye bath** can give different colours depending on what you add, and a colour can shift later if the cloth is washed in alkaline soap.

## Dye, pigment and fibre

- A **dye** dissolves in water and soaks into the fibre as single molecules.
- A **pigment** doesn't dissolve; it sits on top, like paint or clay.

Cotton is **cellulose**, a long chain of sugar units covered in –OH groups. Some dyes hold onto cellulose by themselves, through many weak attractions (hydrogen bonds). These are called **direct** or **substantive** dyes. **Tannins**, the bitter, mouth-drying substances in tea, coffee and eucalyptus, cling to cellulose quite well, which is why a tea stain is hard to remove.

Most other plant dyes hold on weakly. The cloth looks coloured, but the dye washes out.

## Mordants

A **mordant** (from French *mordre*, to bite) is a substance that helps the dye bite into the fibre. The most used mordant in history, and the only one we use, is **alum**, potassium aluminium sulfate, $\text{KAl(SO}_4)_2 \cdot 12\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

In water, alum releases aluminium ions, $\text{Al}^{3+}$. Each ion can grab several things at once:

$$\text{fibre} \;-\; \text{Al}^{3+} \;-\; \text{dye}$$

The aluminium sits on the fibre and holds dye molecules to it. The dye–metal complex, called a **lake**, does not dissolve in water. So washing can't easily carry it off. The metal also changes the dye's shape a little, so mordanted colours are often brighter or a slightly different shade.

**Wood-ash water** (አመድ, amed, soaked in water and settled) is strongly alkaline, about pH 10–11, because it contains potassium carbonate. It cleans grease and starch out of cotton and changes the colour of indicator-like dyes. It holds dye much less well than alum, but it is cheap and traditional, so it is worth testing.

Dyers use **chromium, copper and tin** salts too. They are toxic. We never use them.

### How much mordant?

Dyers measure mordant as a percentage of the **weight of fibre** (WOF), the mass of dry cloth:

$$\text{mass of alum} = \frac{\text{WOF \%}}{100} \times \text{mass of dry cloth}$$

**Worked example.** For 30 g of cotton at 15% WOF: $0.15 \times 30 = 4.5$ g of alum.

## Fastness: does the colour last?

A colour is **fast** if it stays. There are three common tests:

- **Wash fastness**: wash with soap and rub, several times.
- **Light fastness**: leave in strong sunlight. Ultraviolet light carries enough energy to break bonds in the chromophore, so the molecule stops absorbing visible light and the colour fades.
- **Rub fastness**: rub against white cloth and see how much colour comes off.

Each test needs a **control**: a piece of the same dyed cloth that wasn't washed, or was covered from the light.

### Scoring by eye

Industry uses a **grey scale** from 5 to 1:

| Score | Change compared with the control |
|---|---|
| 5 | no visible change |
| 4 | slight change |
| 3 | clear change |
| 2 | big change |
| 1 | almost all the colour gone |

### Scoring with numbers

Take a phone photo of the swatch next to its control and a piece of **undyed** cloth, all in the same picture, in shade, not direct sun. A free colour-picker app reads the red, green and blue values (0–255) at a point. Brightness is the mean:

$$\text{brightness} = \frac{R + G + B}{3}$$

The **darkness** of a swatch is how much darker it is than undyed cloth in the same photo:

$$D = \text{brightness}_{\text{undyed}} - \text{brightness}_{\text{swatch}}$$

and the colour kept after a test is

$$\text{colour kept (\%)} = \frac{D_{\text{after}}}{D_{\text{before}}} \times 100$$

**Worked example.** Undyed cloth reads 225. The unwashed control reads (170, 125, 70), brightness 121.7, so $D = 103.3$. The washed swatch reads (185, 145, 95), brightness 141.7, so $D = 83.3$. Colour kept $= 83.3 \div 103.3 \times 100 \approx 81\%$.

Always compare swatches **in the same photo**: phones change the brightness of each photo automatically.

## Depth of shade

More dye in the bath gives a deeper shade, but not without limit. Cotton has only so many places for dye (or for aluminium) to attach. At low concentrations, doubling the dye nearly doubles the darkness. At high concentrations, the places fill up and the curve **levels off**, so extra dye is wasted and stays in the bath. A good dyer finds the concentration just before the curve flattens.
