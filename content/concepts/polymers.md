Some molecules are tiny: water has just three atoms. Others are giants made of thousands of small units joined end to end, like beads on a necklace. These are **polymers** (from Greek: *poly* = many, *mer* = part).

## Monomers and polymers

The small unit is a **monomer**. Joining many monomers makes a **polymer**.

| Polymer | Monomer | Where you find it |
|---|---|---|
| Starch | glucose | teff, maize, potatoes, cassava |
| Cellulose | glucose | cotton, paper, wood, plant cell walls |
| Protein | amino acids | muscle, egg white, wool, silk |
| DNA | nucleotides | every cell |
| Polythene | ethene | plastic bags, bottles |
| Nylon | two kinds of monomer | ropes, clothes |

Starch and cellulose are both made of glucose, but joined differently. That small difference means you can digest starch but not cellulose.

## Why chains matter

A material's properties come from how its chains behave:

- **Long chains tangle** together like cooked spaghetti, which makes polymers tough.
- **Forces between chains** hold them together. Stronger forces make the material stiffer.
- **Chains that can slide** past each other make a material stretchy or bendy.
- **Cross-links** (bonds tying chains together) make a material harder and stop it melting.

## Plasticisers

A **plasticiser** is a small molecule that gets between the chains and pushes them apart, so they slide more easily. More plasticiser makes a material softer and more flexible, but weaker. Glycerol (glycerine) is a plasticiser for starch plastics. Without it, dried starch is hard and brittle.

## Making a starch bioplastic

1. Mix starch, water, a little vinegar and some glycerol.
2. Heat while stirring. The starch grains swell and burst, releasing their chains (this is **gelatinisation**, the same thing that thickens a sauce). The mixture turns thick and see-through.
3. Spread it thin and let it dry. As the water leaves, the chains tangle and bond to each other, forming a film.

The vinegar helps break up some of the branched starch chains, which gives a more even plastic.

## Testing polymers

| Property | How to test | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Tensile strength | hang masses from a strip until it breaks; divide the force by the strip's cross-section area | Pa (N/m²) |
| Flexibility | bend it around rods of smaller and smaller diameter until it cracks | smallest diameter (mm) |
| Water resistance | soak a weighed piece; weigh again | % mass gained |
| Biodegradability | bury pieces in damp soil; dig up and weigh weekly | % mass lost |

## The plastic problem

Most plastics are made from oil and take decades or centuries to break down. They block drains, harm animals and break into tiny pieces (microplastics). **Bioplastics** made from starch or cellulose can be broken down by microbes, which have the enzymes to digest those chains. But they are usually weaker and absorb water, so they suit some uses (food wrapping, plant pots) better than others.
