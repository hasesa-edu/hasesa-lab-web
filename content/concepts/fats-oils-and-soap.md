Water alone won't wash grease off a plate or your hands: oil and water don't mix. Soap is the go-between, and people have been making it from fat and ashes for over 4,000 years.

## What oils and fats are

Fats (solid at room temperature) and oils (liquid) are the same kind of molecule: a **triglyceride**. It's one **glycerol** molecule with three long **fatty-acid** chains attached by ester links.

- The chains are mostly carbon and hydrogen (**hydrocarbons**). They don't mix with water.
- Chains with only single bonds pack neatly, making solid fats (butter, coconut oil, palm oil).
- Chains with some double bonds have kinks, pack poorly, and make liquid oils (sunflower, olive, nug/niger seed).

## Saponification: making soap

Heat or mix a fat with a strong **alkali** such as sodium hydroxide (lye, caustic soda), and the ester links break:

$$\text{fat (triglyceride)} + 3\,\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\ \text{soap molecules} + \text{glycerol}$$

This reaction is **saponification** ("soap-making"). Sodium hydroxide gives hard soaps; potassium hydroxide gives soft and liquid soaps. Traditionally, people used the potassium-rich alkali leached from wood ash.

## How soap cleans

A soap molecule is long and thin with two very different ends:

- a **head** with an electric charge, which loves water (**hydrophilic**),
- a long hydrocarbon **tail**, which loves oil (**hydrophobic**).

In water with grease, soap molecules push their tails into the grease and keep their heads in the water. They surround each grease droplet in a ball called a **micelle**, which floats off into the rinse water. The oil and water now form a stable **emulsion**.

## Soap and hard water

In hard water (water with dissolved calcium and magnesium, common in some boreholes), soap reacts with these ions to make a grey, insoluble **scum** instead of a lather. Detergents (synthetic soaps) don't form scum, which is why they're used in washing powders.

## Safety: lye is dangerous

Sodium hydroxide is strongly **corrosive**. It dissolves skin and can blind. When it's dissolved in water, the solution gets hot (about 80–90 °C) and gives off irritating fumes. In a lab or a soap workshop:

- goggles, gloves and long sleeves,
- always add **lye to water**, never water to lye,
- work with good airflow,
- if it touches skin: rinse with plenty of running water for at least 15 minutes,
- if it gets in an eye: rinse for at least 15 minutes and get medical help immediately.

## Curing

Saponification goes on for a day or two after the soap is poured into moulds. Over the next **4–6 weeks** the bar dries and hardens. Testing the pH before use is essential: a well-made, cured soap is around pH 9–10. A soap above about pH 10–11 has unreacted lye and must not be used on skin.
