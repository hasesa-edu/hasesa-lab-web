Microbes (micro-organisms) are living things so small that you need a microscope to see one. They are everywhere: in soil, water, air, on your skin and inside your gut. A teaspoon of soil holds more microbes than there are people on Earth.

## The main kinds

| Kind | Typical size | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Viruses | 0.02–0.3 µm | rotavirus, measles, flu |
| Bacteria | 0.5–5 µm | cholera, typhoid (harmful); the lactic acid bacteria in irgo and injera (helpful) |
| Fungi (single-celled) | 3–10 µm | baker's yeast |
| Protozoa | 10–50 µm | *Giardia*, amoebas |
| Worm eggs | 30–100 µm | roundworm |

1 µm (micrometre) is a thousandth of a millimetre. A grain of fine sand is about 100 µm across; a clay particle that makes water cloudy is about 1–2 µm.

## Helpful and harmful

Most microbes don't harm us, and many are essential:

- Yeast and bacteria ferment teff batter into injera, milk into irgo, and barley into tella.
- Bacteria in the soil break down dead plants and return their nutrients.
- Bacteria in your gut help digest food.

A few are **pathogens**: they cause disease. Diarrhoea from dirty water is one of the biggest causes of illness in young children worldwide.

## How fast they grow

Bacteria reproduce by splitting in two. In warm, wet conditions with food, some divide every 20 minutes. Each new cell splits too, so the number **doubles** each time:

$$N = N_0 \times 2^{n}$$

where $N_0$ is the starting number and $n$ is the number of doublings. After 6 doublings (2 hours), 1 becomes 64. After 30 doublings (10 hours), more than a billion. That's why food left out on a hot day spoils so fast.

## What microbes need, and what stops them

Most need **water**, **food**, the right **temperature** (many grow best around 25–40 °C) and the right **pH**. Take one away and they slow down or die:

- **Heat:** boiling kills bacteria, viruses and protozoa.
- **Chemicals:** chlorine, alcohol and soap damage their outer layers.
- **Ultraviolet light:** UV in sunlight damages their DNA.
- **Acid:** sour injera batter and irgo are too acidic for many harmful bacteria.
- **Cold:** a fridge slows them down but doesn't kill them.

## How germs get into water

The most common route is **faecal–oral**: germs leave a sick person or animal in faeces, reach water through rain, rivers or unwashed hands, and then someone drinks the water or eats food washed in it. Breaking this chain anywhere (toilets, handwashing, protecting springs, treating water) stops disease.
