A **pure substance** has only one kind of particle: pure water, pure sugar (sucrose), pure salt. A **mixture** has two or more substances mingled together but not chemically joined. Almost every food is a mixture, and that is exactly what makes faking possible: a faker just mixes in one more thing.

## Kinds of mixtures

- **Solution**: one substance dissolves completely in another. The particles are single molecules or ions, so the liquid is clear and never settles. Example: sugar syrup. Honey is mostly a very concentrated solution of sugars in water (about 17% water).
- **Suspension**: particles big enough to see, spread through a liquid, that **settle** if you leave it. Example: berbere stirred into water.
- **Colloid**: particles in between: too small to settle quickly, but big enough to scatter light. Example: milk, which is white because its fat droplets scatter light.

A mixture is **homogeneous** if it looks the same everywhere (solutions, and colloids to the eye) and **heterogeneous** if you can see different parts (suspensions).

## Separating mixtures

Because the parts of a mixture keep their own properties, we can use those differences to pull them apart.

**Sedimentation.** Dense, insoluble particles sink. How fast depends on their density and size: big, dense grains of brick dust (about 2.6 g/cm³) drop in seconds. Fine spice particles, which hold oil and trapped air, float or hang about for many minutes.

**Decanting.** Once something has settled, pour the liquid off gently and leave the sediment behind.

**Filtration.** Pour the mixture through filter paper. Undissolved solids stay on the paper (the **residue**); the liquid and anything dissolved in it pass through (the **filtrate**).

**Evaporation.** Heat or leave a solution so that the water leaves, and the dissolved solid stays behind.

**Chromatography.** Separates dissolved coloured substances by how far they travel up paper. (It has its own unit.)

## Worked example: how much grit?

A suspect berbere: 10.0 g stirred in water, sediment collected and dried: 2.3 g. A genuine berbere control, done the same way: 0.3 g (real spices carry a little sand from the field).

Extra sediment: $2.3 - 0.3 = 2.0$ g.

$$\text{added grit} \approx \frac{2.0}{10.0} \times 100 = 20\%$$

## Concentration

For a solution, **percentage by mass** is

$$\% = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100$$

Dissolve 30 g sugar in 70 g water: $\frac{30}{100} \times 100 = 30\%$. Always divide by the total mass of the solution.
