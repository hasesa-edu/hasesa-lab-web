Every substance carries clues about what it is. Chemists call these clues **properties**. A market detective learns to read them.

## Physical properties

A **physical property** can be observed or measured without turning the substance into something else.

- **Appearance**: colour, shine, whether it is clear or cloudy.
- **Smell**: honey smells of flowers; sugar syrup smells of almost nothing.
- **Texture**: berbere should feel fine and soft; brick dust feels gritty between your fingers (feel it, never taste it).
- **State**: solid, liquid or gas at room temperature. Kibe is soft and solid when cool, a clear liquid when warm.
- **Density**: how much mass fits in each millilitre.
- **Solubility**: whether it dissolves in water, in oil, or in neither.
- **Viscosity**: how thick and slow a liquid flows. Real honey pours like a slow rope.

## Chemical properties

A **chemical property** describes how a substance can change into a *new* substance.

- Milk turns sour: bacteria turn its sugar (lactose) into lactic acid.
- Starch turns blue-black with iodine: the two form a new coloured complex.
- Baking soda fizzes with acid: it makes carbon dioxide gas.

Chemical tests use chemical properties to reveal hidden ingredients.

## Two kinds of properties

Some properties depend on **how much** you have: mass and volume. These are called **extensive**.

Others are the same for a spoon or a barrel: density, colour, boiling point, pH. These are called **intensive**. Intensive properties are the best fingerprints, because the size of your sample does not matter.

**Worked example.** A trader's jar holds 700 g of honey, and your test spoon holds 14 g. If the honey is all the same, both have the same density, maybe 1.40 g/mL. But the jar has 50 times the mass and 50 times the volume.

## Hidden but not gone

When sugar dissolves in water it vanishes from sight, but its mass stays. Dissolve 5.0 g of sugar in 100.0 g of water and the solution weighs 105.0 g. This is **conservation of mass**: in physical and chemical changes, no mass is created or destroyed.

That is good news for detectives. A faker can hide an ingredient from your eyes, but not from the scale, the beaker of water, or a chemical test.

## Observing like a scientist

- Write what you **see**, not what you think. "Cloudy, with white specks at the bottom" is an observation. "Has flour in it" is a conclusion, which comes later.
- Compare against a **known genuine** sample whenever you can.
- Use your nose by **wafting**: hold the sample away from your face and wave the air towards you with your hand.
- **Never taste** a lab sample.
