Dig a hole in a school field in Addis Ababa and you find red-brown or black earth. Pour water on it and knead it, and it becomes **chika** (ጭቃ, mud): the material of millions of Ethiopian homes. Why does some earth make strong walls, while other earth crumbles or cracks? The answer is in what soil is made of.

## What is soil?

Soil is a **mixture**. It contains:

- **mineral particles**: bits of broken-down rock, of many sizes,
- **organic matter**: rotted leaves, roots and animals (the dark stuff in topsoil),
- **water** and **air** in the gaps between particles.

For building, we care about the mineral particles. Builders dig **below** the dark topsoil, because organic matter rots and makes walls weak.

## Sand, silt and clay

Soil scientists sort mineral particles by size:

| Particle | Size | How it feels when wet | How fast it settles in water |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sand** (ashewa, አሸዋ) | 0.05–2 mm, you can see the grains | gritty | seconds |
| **Silt** | 0.002–0.05 mm | smooth, like flour or talc | minutes to hours |
| **Clay** | smaller than 0.002 mm (2 µm) | sticky and shiny; you can mould it | days; some never settles |

A soil's **texture** is how much of each it has. A soil with roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay is called a **loam**. A soil with more than about 40% clay is a **clay soil**.

## Finding the texture

**By feel.** Wet a lump and rub it between your fingers. Gritty means sand. Silky means silt. Sticky means clay.

**The ball and ribbon tests.** Roll a wet ball. If it falls apart, there is little clay. If it holds, squeeze it out between your thumb and finger into a flat ribbon. The longer the ribbon grows before it breaks, the more clay:

| Ribbon length | Clay |
|---|---|
| no ribbon | under about 10% |
| under 2.5 cm | about 10–25% |
| 2.5–5 cm | about 25–40% |
| over 5 cm | over about 40% |

**The jar test.** Shake soil with water (and a little detergent to stop the clay clumping) in a straight-sided jar, then let it stand. Big, heavy sand grains sink first, then silt, then clay. Measure each layer:

$$\text{percentage of clay} = \frac{\text{thickness of clay layer}}{\text{total thickness}} \times 100$$

The layers are rough, because some silt and clay settle together, but the test is good enough to choose a soil for building.

## How clay glues

Clay is not just very fine sand. It is a different kind of mineral. Its particles are thin, flat **plates**, like a pile of microscopic playing cards.

- Because they are so small and flat, a handful of clay has an enormous surface area: a single teaspoon of clay has as much surface as a classroom floor, and some clays far more.
- The plates carry a small negative electric charge. Water molecules (and dissolved positive ions) stick to their surfaces in thin films.
- These water films act like glue between the plates. Wet two smooth tiles and press them together and you feel the same effect: they slide, but are hard to pull apart.

So wet clay is **plastic**: you can shape it and it keeps its shape. As it dries, the plates are pulled tightly together, and the clay sets hard. Clay is the **binder** in a mud brick, like cement in concrete. Sand is the **filler**: hard grains that give the brick its body.

## Why mud cracks

The same water films cause trouble. As water leaves, the clay plates move closer and the clay **shrinks**:

$$\text{shrinkage (\%)} = \frac{\text{wet length} - \text{dry length}}{\text{wet length}} \times 100$$

The outside of a brick dries first and tries to shrink, while the wet inside doesn't. The outside is stretched, and mud is weak in tension, so it cracks. More clay means more shrinkage and more cracks.

Some clays are much worse than others. The **black cotton soil** (a vertisol) common around Addis Ababa contains clay that swells hugely when wet and shrinks when dry: that is why it opens wide cracks in the dry season. It makes poor bricks on its own. The **red soils** of many highland areas contain a gentler clay and build better.

## Fixing the mix

Builders have known the answers for thousands of years:

- **Add sand.** Sand grains don't shrink. They take up space, so there is less clay to shrink, and they form a stiff skeleton. Too much sand, though, and there is not enough clay to glue the grains, so the brick crumbles. A good adobe soil has roughly 15–30% clay.
- **Add fibre.** Chopped straw (in Ethiopia, teff straw, chid ጭድ) bridges across tiny cracks and stops them growing. It also makes the mud lighter and helps it dry evenly.
- **Add a stabiliser.** Fresh cow dung contains fibres and sticky substances that make plaster resist rain. Wood ash and lime (nora, ኖራ) react with clay and make it less likely to soften in water.
- **Dry slowly.** Bricks dried in shade, out of strong sun and wind, crack less, because the inside and outside shrink more evenly.

Making the best mud is a fair test: change one ingredient at a time, keep everything else the same, and measure shrinkage, cracks, strength and resistance to water.
