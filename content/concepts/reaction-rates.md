Iron rusts over months. A match flares in a second. Coffee beans turn brown in ten minutes on a hot pan. How fast a change happens is its **rate**.

## Measuring a rate

The rate of a reaction is how much something changes per unit of time:

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{amount of change}}{\text{time taken}}$$

You can follow a reaction by measuring anything that changes: the mass of a roasting bean, the volume of gas given off, the colour of a solution, or the time until something disappears.

A graph of amount against time is steepest at the start (fastest) and flattens as the reaction slows and stops.

## Collision theory

Particles can only react when they **collide**, and only if they hit hard enough. The minimum energy needed is the **activation energy**. Anything that makes useful collisions happen more often speeds the reaction up.

## The four factors

**1. Temperature.** Hotter particles move faster, so they collide more often, and many more collisions have enough energy. A rough rule: many reactions go about twice as fast for every 10 °C rise.

**2. Concentration (or pressure, for gases).** More particles in the same space means more collisions. A stronger acid reacts faster.

**3. Surface area.** Only particles on the surface of a solid can be hit. Cutting a solid into smaller pieces exposes more surface:

| Pieces from a 1 cm cube | Side of each piece | Total surface |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 cm | 6 cm² |
| 8 | 0.5 cm | 12 cm² |
| 1000 | 0.1 cm | 60 cm² |

That's why powders react (and dissolve) fast, and why fine flour dust can even explode.

**4. Catalysts.** A catalyst offers an easier route with lower activation energy, and is not used up. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

## In the kitchen: roasting coffee

When green coffee beans are heated on a menkeshkesh (roasting pan):

1. **Drying** (up to about 160 °C): water inside turns to steam. The beans turn yellow and smell grassy.
2. **Browning** (about 160–200 °C): sugars and proteins react with each other (the **Maillard reaction**) and sugars caramelise. Hundreds of new flavour and colour molecules form. These reactions speed up enormously as the temperature rises.
3. **First crack** (around 196 °C): steam and carbon dioxide build up inside and split the bean with a pop. It swells.
4. Roasting longer gives a **darker** roast: more bitter, less acidic, and lighter in mass.

Because rate depends so strongly on temperature, a few degrees hotter can turn a good roast into a burnt one in under a minute. That's why roasters keep the beans moving.

## Dissolving and extraction

Dissolving isn't a chemical reaction, but it follows the same rules: hotter water, stirring and a finer grind all speed it up. In brewing, this means time, temperature and grind size have to be balanced together.
