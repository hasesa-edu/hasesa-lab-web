Stand at the edge of the Rift Valley near Adama or on the escarpment road down to the Afar, and you are looking at a continent coming apart.

## Plates

The Earth's outer shell is broken into huge slabs called **tectonic plates**, about 100 km thick, which float on hotter, softer rock below and move a few centimetres a year at most, about as fast as your fingernails grow.

Where plates meet, things happen:

| Boundary | Plates | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divergent | pull apart | rift valleys, volcanoes, new crust | the East African Rift, the Red Sea |
| Convergent | push together | mountains, deep earthquakes | the Himalayas |
| Transform | slide past | earthquakes | the San Andreas Fault (USA) |

## The East African Rift

Under Ethiopia, the African plate is splitting into two parts, the **Nubian** plate to the west and the **Somali** plate to the east, moving apart at about 5–7 mm a year. In the Afar, a third plate, the **Arabian** plate, is pulling away too: it's one of the few places on land where three plates meet.

As the crust stretches, it cracks along **faults** and blocks drop down to form the valley floor, with the Rift Valley lakes along it. Hot magma rises through the thin crust, feeding volcanoes and hot springs (like those at Sodere and Wondo Genet). In 2005, at Dabbahu in the Afar, a crack about 60 km long opened by several metres in a few weeks.

## Earthquakes

Rocks on either side of a fault are locked together by friction while the plates keep pulling. Strain builds up for years, until the rock suddenly slips. The stored energy spreads out as **seismic waves**: that's an earthquake. In early 2025, a swarm of earthquakes near Mount Fentale and the Awash area, up to about magnitude 5.8, was felt in Addis Ababa.

## Seismic waves

| Wave | Type | Speed in crust | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P wave** (primary) | longitudinal: pushes and pulls in the direction of travel | about 6 km/s | arrives first, a sudden jolt |
| **S wave** (secondary) | transverse: shakes side to side | about 3.5 km/s | arrives second, stronger shaking |
| **Surface waves** | roll along the surface | slower | most damage to buildings |

Because S waves are slower, the gap between the P and S arrivals tells you how far away the earthquake was. A rough rule: **distance (km) ≈ 8 × gap (s)**.

## Measuring earthquakes

A **seismometer** records ground movement as a wiggly line, a **seismogram**.

- **Magnitude** measures the size of the earthquake itself. Each step up means about 10 times more ground movement and about 32 times more energy. A magnitude 7 releases about 1000 times the energy of a magnitude 5.
- **Intensity** describes the shaking and damage at a particular place, which depends on distance, the ground and the buildings.

## Why buildings fall down

Earthquakes rarely kill people directly. **Collapsing buildings** do. The ground shakes **sideways** much more than buildings are designed for (they're built mainly to hold up their own weight). Whether a building survives depends on its shape, bracing, materials and how its natural frequency compares with the frequency of the shaking.
